A bridge isn't competent because the blueprint is elegant, but because the bridge holds — and continues to hold when trucks cross, winds rise, and inspectors check the bolts.

Joan Tronto insists that "assuming responsibility is not yet the same as doing the actual work of care." Chapter 4 bound the promises; Chapter 5 asks whether the system actually delivers on them. Competence is about execution: working code that does what it promised, audited, explainable, and safe-to-fail. And crucially — a point Tronto presses on technologists in particular — "to be competent to care is not simply a technical issue, but a moral one." A system that ships broken care with good intentions has failed morally, not just technically. The promise was made; the bridge did not hold.

The framing we carry forward from the preceding chapter is this: we check the process. Not "just trust us," but transparency and fast operational feedback on how care is delivered. Trust, in a Civic AI context, is not a disposition you extend once to a vendor; it is something earned incrementally, through demonstrated, inspectable practice.

### What Competence Actually Means

The word "competence" invites misreading. In ordinary usage it tends to describe an attribute — a person or system either has competence or lacks it. In Tronto's framework, and in our application of it to Civic AI, competence is a property of practice: something demonstrated continuously in operation, not assumed from design documentation or inferred from a model card. A system is competent the way a bridge is sound — not because an engineer once certified it, but because it has been tested under load, inspected on a schedule, and found to hold under the conditions it will actually face.

This has immediate implications for how we build and release Civic AI systems. Safety must be something people can inspect in operation, not infer from vendor intent. Consider what happens when we forget that.

In 2023 a metropolitan social-services authority deployed an AI triage tool to allocate home-care hours to elderly residents. The system had looked impressive on paper: benchmark accuracy above ninety per cent, a clean security review, a vendor demo in which the AI walked staff through its reasoning. What no one had established was whether the benchmark population matched the authority's actual caseload — it did not, heavily under-representing residents with multiple chronic conditions — and what no one had built was a way to see the system's reasoning on live cases. Within six weeks, caseworkers were noticing that the hour allocations felt wrong for their most complex clients, but they had no mechanism to inspect why. Concerns escalated informally. They were not acted on quickly enough. The eventual audit found systematic underestimation of need for residents with three or more comorbidities; dozens had received fewer hours than a human would have recommended. Some had deteriorated. Two years of procurement effort, and real harm to real people, because four disciplines had been skipped.

Those four disciplines are the ones we build every subsequent section of this chapter around. First, proof before promotion: features should graduate through a sequence of deployment stages — shadow mode, canary release, general rollout with guardrails — rather than being pushed live because a benchmark looks good. Second, observability over opaqueness: a "show your work" approach, with decision traces, source citations, uncertainty scores, and explainable summaries tied to individual decisions, is the baseline requirement for any system that acts on behalf of citizens. Observability here means the system's reasoning is inspectable, not that anyone can see individual private interactions; privacy and transparency are not in conflict. Third, least power: the simplest mechanism adequate to the need should be used, because complexity grows attack surface. Fourth, fail safely: when evidence is weak, when a component drifts from expected behaviour, or when confidence drops below a threshold, the system narrows scope, hands off to a human, or pauses — rather than extrapolating and bluffing.

Each of these properties connects directly to what Chapter 4 established. The Engagement Contract defined the promises; competence is the discipline by which those promises are kept visible. A decision trace is the operational counterpart of the contract: it shows, for any given action, which rule fired, which sources were consulted, and what uncertainty the system carried. Without it, the contract is a document; with it, the contract lives in the daily operation of the system.

### The Public Master Model

Competence in Civic AI finds its most useful grounding not in software engineering metaphors — though those matter — but in a concept drawn from artisanal craft tradition: the Public Master (公共師傅, _gōnggòng shīfu_).

In many parts of Taiwan, and in similar craft economies elsewhere, the master craftsperson occupies a distinctive social role. Consider the precision CNC machine tool shops concentrated in Taichung's industrial districts. The machinists who operate these shops have spent years developing an intimate relationship with their machines — they know from the sound of the spindle whether a tool is about to chatter, can tell from the texture of a cut whether the coolant flow is slightly off, and carry in their hands and ears a kind of knowledge that cannot be fully written down. This is _mohui_ (默會知識) — tacit knowledge, knowledge that has been embodied through practice rather than encoded in manuals.

When we imagine an AI apprentice placed in that workshop, two things must be true for the relationship to work safely. The machinist steers: she decides which cut to take, when to slow the feed rate, whether the latest proposal from the AI co-pilot is worth acting on or should be set aside. The AI's role is to make that decision better-informed — surfacing tool-life data, flagging a statistical uptick in chatter frequency on similar materials — but actual execution remains under her direction. This is the steering wheel, and it is not optional. An AI co-pilot that can override the operator has inverted the care relationship: the apprentice is now directing the master. Equally important is what the machinist can do the moment something feels wrong: reach for a single, prominent control and stop the machine now, without navigating a menu, without waiting for a confirmation dialogue. The brake must be wired directly, tested regularly, and available at the speed of human recognition — the speed at which an experienced machinist notices that a cut has gone wrong and her hand is already moving. A brake that has not been tested is a brake whose reliability is unknown; a brake buried three levels deep in a software interface is not a brake at all.

The same quality of tacit knowledge appears in bedside nursing. A nurse who has spent years on a particular ward develops a feel for patients that no monitoring system can fully replicate. She knows this patient's baseline breathing rhythm well enough to hear, at three in the morning, the subtle difference between ordinary sleep-apnoea noise and something that warrants waking the doctor. That knowledge is not in the chart. It lives in her ears and in the accumulated pattern-matching of hundreds of previous nights.

For an AI system to be a genuine apprentice on that ward rather than a liability, the nurse needs two further guarantees. She needs the maintenance manual: clear traceability of the AI's logic, including which specific rules fired, which training inputs guided the output, and who is accountable for repairing the model when it fails. A master craftsperson can maintain a machine only if she understands how it works — not in full engineering detail necessarily, but at the level of this component does this, this is what goes wrong when it fails, this is how to tell the difference. A Civic AI system whose reasoning is opaque to its operators is a system that can only be maintained by the original vendor. That creates a structural dependency that undermines the entire care framework: the nurse cannot contest an AI recommendation she cannot interrogate, and the ward cannot improve a process it cannot see. She also needs the right to exit — the freedom to walk away. Users and communities must be able to port their accumulated interaction history, custom settings, and fine-tuned weights when moving to another software environment. This is the exit right that Chapter 4's Engagement Contract commits to in principle; the maintenance manual and the portability of trained weights are what make it real in practice. Vendor lock-in is not merely a commercial inconvenience — it is a care relationship from which one cannot withdraw, which Tronto would recognise as precisely the kind of structural dependency that the care ethics framework exists to resist.

The Public Master model asks: what would it look like to deploy AI as a digital apprentice alongside these human masters, rather than as a replacement for them? The apprentice works in shadow mode at first — observing, recording decision traces, building up a model of the context-specific, tacit knowledge that the master brings to bear. It does not act; it learns, and it makes its learning visible. Over time, if the shadow-mode comparisons show that the apprentice's proposals consistently match or usefully extend what the master would have done, it may graduate to making suggestions. But the master steers — and carries the brake, the maintenance manual, and the key to the door.

### Shadow Mode, Canary, and General Release

The Public Master model gives us the philosophical frame; the deployment sequence gives us the operational one. These are not separate concerns. The sequence of shadow mode, canary release, and general rollout with guardrails is how the apprenticeship model is enacted in software.

Shadow mode means the system sees real inputs and proposes real actions, but does not act. Its proposals are compared to what the human operator or the previous system would have done. Divergences are examined — not to judge the AI wrong, because sometimes it catches something the human missed, but to understand the nature and frequency of disagreements. Shadow mode is the apprentice watching over the master's shoulder. It is also the point at which context-specific tacit knowledge — _mohui_ — begins to be encoded: the system learns the particulars of this workshop, this ward, this community's patterns, before it is trusted to act in them.

Canary release deploys the system to a small, representative slice of real cases — representative is the operative word, and stratifying by demographics and risk profile matters enormously — with automatic rollback triggers if drift exceeds defined bounds. An appeals rate above a threshold, a denial-rate spike in any particular sub-population, a drop in confidence scores: these are the canary's vital signs. The canary stage is where the gap between test performance and operational reality tends to show itself. Hold-out datasets and randomised spot checks guard against train/test leakage — the common failure mode where evaluations look good precisely because they are too similar to the training data.

General rollout happens only after an independent audit: a review of evaluations, logs, and guardrails, conducted by someone with no stake in a clean result, with findings published as an attested report. The audit is not a one-time event; it initiates a monitoring posture that persists through general operation. Drift monitors watch data distribution, performance metrics, and fairness indicators. The pause mechanism — the brake — remains wired and tested throughout.

Post-incident learning closes the loop. When something goes wrong — and in any system operating at scale and over time, something will go wrong — blameless post-mortems examine the failure at the level of mechanism and process, not individual fault. The findings become new tests in the evaluation registry. Each failure, properly examined and properly memorialised, makes the system more competent. This is exactly the logic of the master craftsperson's accumulated knowledge: every time a cut went wrong, every time a machine behaved unexpectedly, that experience was absorbed into the body of _mohui_ that made the master trustworthy.

### Security as a Care Obligation

One aspect of competence that is frequently treated as a purely technical matter — and thereby escaped from the moral framing — is security. We insist it belongs here.

An AI agent with filesystem or network access, running in an inadequately sandboxed environment with more permissions than it needs, is not merely a technical vulnerability. It is a moral failure of care. Prompt injection, privilege escalation, and lateral movement are not technical oversights; they are competence failures that expose the people the system is meant to care for to harms the system's builders accepted implicitly when they chose not to prevent them. The least-privilege principle — using the smallest permission set that meets the operational need — is therefore a care principle, not merely a security heuristic.

The same applies to data minimalism. A system that collects more information than it needs, that retains data past the point of handoff, that fails to honour consent at every stage of the data lifecycle, is not merely non-compliant; it is accumulating an obligation it has not committed to honouring. Every byte of unnecessary retention is a promise of security the system has not made and may not be able to keep.

### The Flood-Bot in Shadow Mode

To make these abstractions concrete, consider how the flood-bot from Chapter 4 enters the competence phase of its deployment. The Engagement Contract has been signed; the brakes have been wired and tested; the oversight board is in place. Now the system needs to prove it can do what it promised.

A new policy — a "medical receipts waiver" for displaced households who cannot produce paper documentation of residence — runs in shadow mode for a week. During that week, the bot sees real incoming livelihood claims and proposes real decisions under the new waiver rule, but does not act on them. Human caseworkers process the same claims in the usual way. The shadow-mode proposals are compared to the human decisions daily. Divergences are examined for patterns: are there demographic groups where the bot and the human systematically disagree? Are there claim types where the bot's confidence is low? Are there any cases where the bot would have made a decision that the caseworkers, after review, regard as clearly wrong?

After a week, if the shadow-mode performance is satisfactory, the waiver policy canaries to ten per cent of livelihood claims — a stratified slice, not a random one, with demographic and geographic balance enforced. The rollback trigger is set at fifteen per cent: if appeals on the new rule exceed that rate, the canary stops automatically, the policy reverts, and the divergences are analysed before any further deployment. Every denial under the waiver carries a decision trace: which rule fired, which sources were consulted, what uncertainty the system carried, and a receipt link that the claimant can use to understand and contest the decision.

When uploaded documents are unreadable — because phone cameras fail in flood conditions, because paper has been soaked — the system does not guess. It uses a reversible default and routes the claim to a human caseworker. This is the brake in operation: not a catastrophic failure, but a principled admission that the system has reached the edge of its competence and must hand off rather than extrapolate.

### The Bridge with Inspection Tags

What the competence framework amounts to, taken whole, is a particular answer to the question of how public trust in AI systems is built and maintained. It is not built by asserting capability, nor by publishing benchmark scores, nor even by demonstrating good intentions. It is built by making the system's operation inspectable in a way that allows trust to be calibrated to evidence.

The closing image for this chapter is the bridge with inspection tags. On well-maintained infrastructure, you will sometimes see plaques or tags recording the date of the last structural inspection, the load tested, and the date of the next inspection. These tags are visible to anyone crossing the bridge. They do not guarantee that the bridge will never fail; nothing does. But they record that someone checked, and will check again, and has committed to checking on a schedule that anyone can verify.

Competence in Civic AI is the presence of those inspection tags on every significant decision the system makes. It is not the absence of failure; it is the presence of proof that the system is being watched, tested, and held to account. The deployment stages — shadow, canary, general with guardrails — are the inspection schedule. The decision traces are the inspection records. The blameless post-mortems are the reports filed when something was found to be wrong. Together, they constitute the practice of competence: not an attribute, but a discipline, maintained continuously, visible to anyone who needs to check.

### What Could Go Wrong

Well-designed systems fail in characteristic ways. The following post-mortem is composite but representative; the pattern it describes has appeared, in recognisable form, in multiple real deployments.

A regional benefits authority went live with an AI decision-support tool after a canary trial that showed a ninety-two per cent match rate with human case officers. Six months into general deployment, a welfare-rights charity filed a complaint: clients from certain postal codes were being denied housing supplements at twice the rate of comparable clients elsewhere. The authority pulled the logs. The first problem was unsafe confidence — the system had been generating plausible-sounding rationale text even for decisions where its internal confidence score was below the threshold at which the designers had intended it to flag for human review. The flag logic had a conditional branch error; the system never escalated. Real households had received denial letters that read authoritative and specific, grounded in reasoning the machine had confected rather than reached. The second problem, discovered when the team examined why the canary had not caught this, was train/test leakage: the canary population had been drawn from the same sampling frame as the training data — urban, digitally active claimants — and had missed the rural and elderly sub-populations among whom the error concentrated. The system had passed its trial on the cases it was already good at.

By the time the audit was complete, several hundred claimants had been incorrectly denied. Some had not appealed — either because the denial letter had seemed authoritative, or because they lacked the literacy or capacity to navigate the appeals process. The human cost was unambiguous and the reputational cost to the authority was severe. The technical fixes — confidence-threshold enforcement, a genuinely stratified canary frame — were straightforward. They had simply not been required.

Beyond these two failures, three further modes deserve attention.

**Opaque reasoning** — the "trust us" explanation — forecloses the possibility of the maintenance manual. If auditors cannot reconstruct how a decision was reached, neither can operators maintain the system, nor can affected communities contest specific decisions. Traceable summaries and public examples are the minimum; auditors need enough to reconstruct the reasoning chain independently.

**Canary bias** — a canary population that is unrepresentative of the general deployment context — means that the canary stage fails to catch failure modes that will appear at scale. Stratified sampling, with published demographics for the canary group, is the fix. A canary that proves performance only on easy cases is not a canary; it is a ceremony.

**Security drift** — permissions that expand quietly over time as the system is extended — turns a once-adequate least-privilege footprint into an attack surface. Regular permission audits, treating scope creep as a care failure rather than an engineering convenience, are the structural fix.

### From Competence to Responsiveness

Chapter 5 has established whether the system did what it promised. Chapter 6 asks whether what it did actually helped — and what happens when it did not.

This handoff is not merely sequential; it is structural. The decision traces that competence produces are the raw material that responsiveness requires. The guardrails-as-code that enforce rights during operation are the same guardrails that define what a valid appeal looks like. The incident loops that post-incident learning establishes feed directly into the community-authored evaluation process that responsiveness makes central. The systems that are well-instrumented, as competence demands, are the systems that responsiveness can actually read.

Competence without responsiveness is a system that checks itself. Responsiveness without competence is a feedback process with nothing reliable to act on. The care cycle requires both.
