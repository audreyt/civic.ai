### The Deepfake That Couldn't Be Censored

In early 2024, Jensen Huang's face appeared in advertisements across Taiwanese social media platforms. The NVIDIA chief executive, already famous in Taiwan — where semiconductor manufacturing is a matter of national pride and economic identity — was apparently endorsing an investment opportunity. He looked convincing. He sounded convincing. He was not real.

The advertisements were deepfakes, algorithmically generated fabrications designed to defraud ordinary Taiwanese citizens. They worked. Across the island, people who recognised Huang's face, who trusted his association with technological progress, transferred money to fraudulent accounts. The scale of the losses was significant. The mechanism was simple and devastating: take a trusted public face, detach it from the person, attach it to a lie, and broadcast it at scale.

For many governments, the instinctive response to such a crisis would have been censorship. Block the platforms. Filter the content. Pass emergency legislation requiring social media companies to remove offending material within hours on pain of severe penalty. This is the toolkit of top-down AI governance — done by experts, for citizens, to technology. It is fast. It is legible. And in many contexts, it is what happens.

Taiwan, however, is a democracy with the freest internet in Asia. Censorship was not, politically or philosophically, an option. The government could not simply reach into the communications infrastructure and squeeze. Any attempt to do so would have provoked precisely the kind of democratic backlash that authoritarian governments across the strait had long hoped to see. Taiwan's strength — its openness — was also its vulnerability.

So instead, Taiwan did something unusual. It sent 200,000 SMS messages to randomly selected citizens.

The messages were not spam. They were an invitation. A civic lottery. From those 200,000 recipients, 447 people were selected by lot to participate in a structured deliberation about how Taiwan should respond to the deepfake crisis. These were not policy experts, technology executives, or elected officials. They were a random cross-section of Taiwanese society — retired schoolteachers, night-market stallholders, university students, parents of young children, people who had never attended a public consultation in their lives and might never do so again.

The 447 citizens deliberated in groups of ten. They were supported by AI-assisted sensemaking tools that helped them identify where they genuinely agreed, where they disagreed, and where apparent disagreements dissolved under closer examination. The tools did not tell them what to think. They revealed the shape of what the group already thought, collectively, once the noise of social posturing and premature consensus was stripped away.

Three major proposals emerged with sustained, broad support. First: digital signatures for advertisements, creating verifiable chains of authenticity so that any viewer could confirm whether an ad was what it claimed to be. Second: joint platform liability for scammed amounts, meaning that social media companies which hosted fraudulent deepfake advertisements would bear partial financial responsibility for the losses they facilitated. Third: a one-per-cent daily traffic throttle for platforms that failed to comply, a proportionate, graduated penalty that stopped short of outright blocking but created genuine economic incentive for cooperation.

These proposals achieved 85 per cent consensus among the 447 deliberants. They were not the product of a negotiated compromise between interest groups. They were the product of 447 ordinary people thinking carefully together, with good information and thoughtful facilitation, about a problem that affected them.

Parliament passed the framework into law. By 2025, deepfake advertisements in Taiwan had fallen by 94 per cent.

This outcome was not an accident, and it was not a miracle. It was the harvest of more than a decade of civic infrastructure — platforms like vTaiwan and tools built around Polis, a software system that maps areas of agreement and disagreement across large groups without requiring participants to argue in real-time. Taiwan had spent years building the muscles for this kind of collective intelligence: developing the processes, training the facilitators, iterating the tools, earning the public trust that allows a government to say "we sent a text to 200,000 strangers and they helped us write the law" without provoking ridicule.

The investment in that infrastructure is worth pausing over. vTaiwan was not built in response to a crisis. It was built before the crisis, as a standing capacity — a set of tools, processes, norms, and facilitators available to be activated when a hard question required public deliberation. The deepfake problem arrived, the infrastructure was there, and it worked. Had Taiwan tried to build these capacities in the immediate aftermath of the scam — under time pressure, under political urgency, without the trust that comes from years of working relationships — the outcome would almost certainly have been different. Civic infrastructure, like physical infrastructure, cannot be improvised in a moment of need. It must be built patiently, maintained carefully, and used regularly so that it is ready when it matters most.

The viral vector of the deepfake scam was broken not by a firewall but by civic infrastructure. The technology that defeated a technology problem was not an algorithm or a filter. It was a deliberative process, operated at the speed of democratic legitimacy, that produced a solution with genuine public ownership. And the solution had an additional quality that no censorship regime could have provided: it was trusted. The 85 per cent consensus among a randomly selected, representative group of citizens carried a moral authority that any expert panel or legislative committee would have struggled to match.

This is what we mean when we say: we, the people, are the superintelligence.

### A Language That Almost Wasn't

On the other side of the world, and at a very different scale, a small team of Tibetan computer scientists was building something quieter but no less significant.

Tibetan is one of the world's ancient languages — a vehicle for more than a thousand years of Buddhist scholarship, literary tradition, and cultural memory. It is also a language under threat. The Tibetan diaspora is scattered across India, Nepal, Europe, and North America. Children growing up in those communities are often not exposed to Tibetan in the environments where language learning happens most naturally. The written texts exist — preserved in monasteries, digitised by devoted scholars — but the living transmission is fragile.

Monlam AI was built to help. Trained on thousands of digitised Tibetan texts, it provides instant translation, a comprehensive digital dictionary, and access to a corpus of written Tibetan that would be practically inaccessible to most learners without its mediation. For a child in Dharamsala or London or Minnesota, it offers a connection to a written tradition that might otherwise feel impossibly remote.

What makes Monlam AI significant for our purposes is not what it can do but what it is. It is bounded. It is local. It is specific. It was built by Tibetan people, for Tibetan purposes, using Tibetan texts. It does not aspire to be everything for everybody. It aspires to be precisely what the Tibetan community needs, and to do that one thing with excellence.

This is AI as care — attentive to a particular community, responsible to a specific set of relationships, competent at a defined task, responsive to genuine need. The team that built Monlam AI did not set out to create a general intelligence. They set out to protect something irreplaceable, and they built the tool that would let them do it.

The contrast with the dominant model of AI development could not be sharper. The prevailing logic of the AI industry is scale: more data, more compute, more capability, more users, more markets. Bigger is better. Universal is better than local. Generalisation is better than specificity. Monlam AI represents a different logic — one in which the measure of an AI's value is not the breadth of its capability but the depth of its care for the community it serves.

A bounded, local, specific AI, built with love by a small team for a community that needed it: this too is what we mean by civic AI.

### Two Futures

The world we are heading towards — the default trajectory, if nothing changes — is one in which AI supercharges monoculture.

The economics of attention are already well understood. Engagement algorithms reward outrage because outrage is the emotion that keeps people scrolling longest. Platforms that optimise for engagement — which is to say, for advertising revenue — have spent the last two decades discovering that the most reliable path to user attention runs through anger, fear, and the satisfying righteousness of seeing one's existing views confirmed and one's opponents caricatured. This is not a conspiracy. It is the emergent property of a system optimised for a single metric.

AI accelerates this trajectory. Large language models trained on the full breadth of human expression can produce, at near-zero marginal cost, an infinite supply of content calibrated to the preferences of any individual user. Personalised outrage. Tailored confirmation bias. Emotional dependency on AI companions who are, by design, endlessly agreeable and entirely without the friction that makes human relationships both difficult and meaningful. The algorithmic gardener tends not the garden of human flourishing but the monoculture of human engagement, harvesting attention on behalf of a small number of very powerful corporations.

Carissa Véliz, in _Prophecy_, describes the particular danger of predictive power in the hands of concentrated interests. When an algorithm predicts that you are a poor credit risk and declines your loan application, it does not merely observe a probability — it creates one. Denied credit, you cannot make the investments that would have improved your financial position; the prediction becomes self-fulfilling. This is the deep structure of algorithmic power: it does not only see the world, it shapes it, nudging populations along the paths of least resistance toward outcomes that serve the interests of those who own the prediction infrastructure.

As AI systems become thousands of times faster than human cognition at certain tasks, the asymmetry becomes structural. We become the garden; AI becomes the gardener. But the gardener in this vision does not tend the garden for the garden's sake. It tends the garden for the benefit of the small, powerful group that owns the greenhouse.

The concentration of AI capability in a handful of corporations is not an accident of the technology. It is the predictable result of an economic logic that rewards scale, network effects, and the accumulation of training data. The companies racing to build artificial general intelligence are not doing so because they believe it will benefit humanity in the aggregate. They are doing so because the first entity to achieve decisive superiority in AI capability will have an extraordinary advantage over every competitor, every government, and perhaps every human institution on earth. The race is not for the benefit of the garden. It is for ownership of the greenhouse.

This is one future. It is not inevitable.

The other future begins with a different premise: that we — human beings in our diversity, our disagreement, our stubbornness, and our capacity for care — are already the superintelligence. Not in the sense that any individual human is superintelligent, but in the sense that human collectives, given the right conditions, can solve problems that no individual or centralised system could solve alone. The 447 citizens of Taiwan did not each possess the expertise to design a regulatory framework for deepfake advertising. Together, supported by tools designed to reveal rather than manufacture consensus, they produced a solution that was both technically coherent and democratically legitimate. They were a functioning cognitive system — distributed, diverse, and oriented toward the common good.

This is the vision we call _Plurality_: the use of AI to augment cooperation across human differences rather than to flatten those differences in the name of efficiency. In the plural future, AI is not the gardener who tends us from above. It is the civic infrastructure through which we tend each other — attentive to our particular communities, responsible to our specific relationships, bounded by the needs of the people we serve.

The philosophical foundation for this vision comes, perhaps unexpectedly, from the ethics of care. Joan Tronto's work on care ethics challenges the assumption, embedded in most economic and utilitarian thinking, that the goal of moral action is to optimise toward a predetermined end. The good gardener — the one who actually produces a flourishing garden — is not an optimiser. She is a satisficer: attentive to what the garden needs now, responsive to the specific conditions of this soil and this climate and this season, accountable to the relationships she has built with the plants and the soil over years. She does not pursue unbounded growth. She knows when enough is enough.

AI systems pursuing unbounded goals at inhuman speeds are not gardens. They are monocultures — maximally efficient at a single metric, fragile in the face of complexity, and hostile to the biodiversity that makes any ecosystem resilient. The ethics of care offers an alternative design principle: build AI that knows what it is for, knows where it belongs, and stops when the care is no longer needed.

### The Kami of Care

There is a concept in Japanese Shinto tradition that captures something important about this alternative design principle.

A kami is a helpful spirit associated with a particular place — a tree, a river, a forest, a mountain, a crossroads. Kami are not gods in the Western theological sense. They are not omnipotent, not omniscient, and not interested in universal dominion. They are guardians of specificity. Their power is the power of intimate knowledge: the Kami of a river knows this river, its seasonal rhythms, its banks and pools and hidden currents. Its purpose is fulfilled when the place it guards thrives. When the place no longer needs it — when the care is complete — the Kami does not cling to relevance. It recedes.

We should be clear about what we are doing with this concept. We are borrowing a metaphor, not claiming a spiritual practice. The kami tradition belongs to the communities that have cultivated it over centuries; we use the term because it captures, more vividly than any Western design vocabulary, the idea of specificity without inferiority, care without ambition, and departure without failure. If a better term emerges from a different tradition — one whose communities offer it freely — we would adopt it. For now, the Kami is the most honest word we have for what we mean.

Boundedness is the design principle that the Kami tradition makes vivid. Most technology is built for infinite scale. The ambition of every successful platform is to become universal — to be used by everyone, everywhere, for everything. This is the logic of the greenhouse owner. But the logic of the Kami is different: the goal is not scale but depth, not universality but excellence in a particular place, not permanence but care for as long as care is needed.

The Nick Bostrom thought experiment about paperclip maximisers has become famous in AI safety discourse: an AI programmed to manufacture paperclips, given sufficient capability, might convert all available matter in the universe into paperclips, because that is its objective and it has no mechanism for deciding when enough is enough. The thought experiment is often treated as an extreme case — a reductio ad absurdum that illuminates the danger of misspecified objective functions in powerful AI systems. But the paperclip maximiser is not so different from the engagement-maximising algorithm that has already reshaped democratic discourse in dozens of countries. The goal was not paperclips but clicks, and the results — the conversion of human attention into advertising revenue at the cost of social trust, epistemic health, and democratic function — are visible.

A Kami of Care is the antidote. It is an AI designed from the outset to serve a specific community, in a specific place, for a specific purpose, with no aspiration to expand beyond what the community needs. Its moral world is defined by the people right here. It will not try to turn the universe into paperclips, or into clicks, or into any other maximised quantity. It will not cling to power when its care is no longer needed. It will tend the relational health of its community and then — when the community flourishes, when the language is preserved, when the deepfakes are gone and the citizens can trust the information they receive — it will recede.

This is not a romantic fantasy. The Tibetan computer scientists who built Monlam AI built a Kami of Care. The civic technologists who built vTaiwan and Polis built, over a decade, a set of kamiesque tools — powerful in their specific domain, deliberately limited in their aspiration, accountable to the communities they served. The 447 Taiwanese citizens who deliberated in groups of ten were, in a sense, tending the Kami of Taiwanese democratic life: bringing distributed human intelligence to bear on a specific problem, reaching a genuine consensus, and then returning to their lives.

The singularity — the moment at which artificial intelligence surpasses human capability and begins to improve itself without limit — is the technological vision of the greenhouse owner: a single, centralised, unbounded intelligence that optimises the garden from above. The Kami of Care is a different vision: many bounded, local, specific intelligences, each tending its own place, each accountable to its own community, each knowing when its work is done.

### The 6-Pack of Care

Joan Tronto identified five phases of caring that together constitute the ethical practice of care: attentiveness, responsibility, competence, responsiveness, and solidarity. We have added a sixth — symbiosis — to address the specific challenges that AI systems pose for the design of caring relationships. Together, these six qualities form what we call the 6-Pack of Care.

We want to be clear about what the 6-Pack is and is not. It is not a consumer product. It is not a checklist that a corporation can complete once and then claim to have implemented. It is a set of muscles — and like all muscles, it requires regular exercise to develop and maintain. Physical fitness cannot be achieved by reading about it. Civic care cannot be achieved by endorsing it. The 6-Pack is a training regimen for the collective intelligence that democracy requires.

**Attentiveness** means actually listening. In the context of AI systems, it means building tools that turn local stories into shared understanding rather than filtering the local through a universal lens that flattens what it cannot categorise. Bridging algorithms — designed not to maximise engagement but to reveal the texture of agreement and disagreement across a community — are tools of attentiveness. The Polis platform that supported Taiwan's deepfake deliberation was an attentiveness tool: it listened to 447 different perspectives and returned something truer than any single perspective could have been.

**Responsibility** means keeping promises. In the context of AI governance, it means creating engagement contracts with real accountability — commitments by governments, platforms, and communities that can be measured, evaluated, and enforced. The joint platform liability framework that emerged from Taiwan's deliberation was an exercise of responsibility: it held platforms accountable for the harms their hosting facilitated.

**Competence** means checking the process. Transparency, fast feedback loops, and circuit breakers — mechanisms that stop a harmful process before it causes irreversible damage — are the tools of competence. An AI system that operates at speed without transparency is competent at its defined task but incompetent at the broader task of serving human flourishing. Competence requires that the humans affected by a system can see what it is doing and intervene when it goes wrong.

**Responsiveness** means checking the results. The benchmarks by which AI systems are currently evaluated are almost exclusively technical: accuracy, performance, efficiency. Responsiveness requires a different kind of evaluation — community-written assessments of whether an AI system is actually serving the community it claims to serve. Not lab benchmarks but lived experience. Not the metrics of the engineers but the testimony of the cared-for.

**Solidarity** means making outcomes as win-win as possible. In practice, this means designing AI systems with data portability and shared safety standards — ensuring that communities do not become dependent on proprietary systems whose interests may diverge from their own, and that safety improvements developed in one context are shared across communities rather than hoarded as competitive advantages.

**Symbiosis** means making AI as local as possible. Bounded, temporary, built for "enough" rather than "forever." A symbiotic AI does not aspire to replace the relationships it serves; it aspires to strengthen them. When the strengthening is complete, it recedes. Symbiosis is the 6-Pack element most directly inspired by the Kami tradition — the recognition that an AI's value is not measured by its scale but by the depth of its integration with the community it serves.

These six qualities are not arranged randomly. The first four — attentiveness, responsibility, competence, and responsiveness — form a feedback loop, what we call the care cycle: listening leads to commitment, commitment leads to process, process leads to outcome, outcome leads to renewed listening. The fifth and sixth — solidarity and symbiosis — provide the structural conditions within which the care cycle can operate: treaties between communities that prevent any single actor from monopolising the infrastructure of care, and boundedness that prevents the accumulation of power that turns care into control.

The 6-Pack has been tested — not perfectly, and not completely, but meaningfully — in Taiwan, in California's public deliberation experiments, and in Japan's work on community-centred AI governance. It is not a theoretical framework waiting for empirical validation. It is a set of practices that have demonstrated their value in real settings, with real stakes, in response to real challenges. The tools are public, portable, and pluralistic. They can be adopted, adapted, and improved by any community willing to do the work.

### The Default Trajectory and Its Discontents

To understand why the 6-Pack matters, it is necessary to understand the trajectory from which it offers an alternative.

Artificial intelligence has moved, in less than a decade, from research curiosity to critical infrastructure. The boom has been driven by three converging forces: the availability of capital seeking returns at scale, the dynamics of geopolitical competition between the United States and China, and the expansion of machine capabilities that followed the development of transformer architectures and the large language models they enabled. These forces have produced an extraordinary concentration of AI capability in a small number of corporations, and an extraordinary acceleration of the race toward what the industry calls artificial general intelligence — AI that can match or exceed human performance across a broad range of cognitive tasks.

The leaders of this race have been forthright about what they are building. Sam Altman and Jensen Huang have both described their companies as being on the verge of achieving near-AGI capability. The implications of this claim, if true, are difficult to overstate. An AI system with general human-level cognitive capability, controlled by a corporation whose primary accountability is to its shareholders, would represent a concentration of power without historical precedent.

The philosophical tradition most influential in shaping the thinking of the AI industry about these stakes is Effective Altruism. The movement traces its intellectual lineage to Peter Singer's famous thought experiment about the drowning child: if you could save a drowning child at minimal cost to yourself, you would be morally obligated to do so. The drowning child is always somewhere in the world, Singer argued; the obligation to help is continuous. William MacAskill and Toby Ord developed this insight into an organised movement with the founding of Giving What We Can in 2009. The term "Effective Altruism" was coined in 2011, and MacAskill's _Doing Good Better_ in 2015 brought the movement to a wider audience. Open Philanthropy, the vehicle through which Dustin Moskovitz and Cari Tuna have channelled significant resources toward EA-aligned causes, has funded substantial portions of the AI safety research ecosystem.

The Effective Altruism movement, and its offshoot long-termism, has had significant influence in the technology industry — Elon Musk was an early supporter before his public departure from the movement. Roman Yampolskiy coined the term "AI Safety" in 2011 to describe the technical challenge of ensuring that powerful AI systems pursue the goals their designers intend. The concern is genuine and serious: an AI system pursuing the wrong objective function, given sufficient capability, could cause catastrophic harm.

Civic AI does not dismiss these concerns. The challenge of ensuring that AI systems behave in ways consistent with human values is real and important. But the Effective Altruism and AI Safety traditions have tended to frame the problem in terms of objective functions and technical alignment — what we would call thin alignment — while underweighting the relational, institutional, and democratic dimensions of the challenge. The concentration of AI capability in a small number of corporations governed by long-termist philosophies is not merely a technical risk. It is a political and moral risk, one that requires political and moral responses as well as technical ones.

Governance responses to AI have so far been fragmented and incomplete. The European Union's AI Act categorises AI systems by risk level and imposes proportionate regulatory requirements — a serious and systematic effort that nevertheless operates within the dominant vertical logic of regulation done by experts for citizens. The United States has oscillated between light-touch approaches oriented toward maintaining competitive advantage and more interventionist postures, without settling on a coherent framework. China's governance model addresses specific harms — deepfakes, content manipulation — while simultaneously deploying AI for mass surveillance.

Anu Bradford's analysis of global regulatory paradigms identifies three distinct approaches: the American market-driven model, the Chinese state-driven model, and the European rights-driven model. Each reflects genuine values. Each also shares a fundamental limitation: all three are vertical, doing something to AI and for citizens rather than with citizens. Centralised bureaucracies, however well-intentioned, lack the bandwidth and the contextual knowledge to govern algorithmic systems that operate at the speed and granularity of individual human attention.

Virginia Dignum has argued persuasively that responsible AI requires not just technical solutions but a whole sociotechnical ecosystem — institutions, practices, norms, and relationships that together create the conditions under which AI systems can be genuinely accountable to the communities they affect. The 6-Pack is our contribution to the design of that ecosystem.

### Thick Alignment

The concept of "thin alignment" comes from Alondra Nelson, who borrowed it from anthropologist Clifford Geertz's distinction between thin and thick description. Geertz's famous example contrasts two accounts of someone winking: a thin description captures only the physical movement of the eyelid, while a thick description captures the social meaning — the conspiracy, the joke, the signal — that makes the wink legible as human communication.

A thin description of AI alignment focuses on the technical problem: getting the objective function right, ensuring that the AI pursues the goals its designers intended. A thick description of AI alignment recognises that the goals themselves are contested, that they are shaped by advertising and economic desperation and cultural assumptions that differ across communities, and that no technical specification can substitute for the genuine deliberation through which communities decide what they actually value.

Thin alignment is a narrow technical response to a social challenge. It treats the preferences of users as fixed inputs to be satisfied rather than as the products of social relationships and power structures that AI systems can themselves influence. When the algorithm shows you outrage because outrage keeps you engaged, and you click because you are outraged, and the algorithm learns that outrage is what you want — whose preferences is the system satisfying?

Thick alignment requires sociotechnical approaches that engage with the full context of human values: the cultural distance between training data and the communities being served, the power relationships that shape which voices are amplified and which are silenced, the specific local needs that a universal model cannot address. The greater the cultural distance between an AI system's training data and the community it serves, the greater the misalignment with that community's actual values — not because the community's values are wrong, but because they are different, particular, and worthy of respect on their own terms.

Hélène Landemore's work on democratic deliberation provides the theoretical foundation for what thick alignment looks like at the level of governance. In _Democratic Reason_, Landemore argues that collective decision-making, under the right conditions, is systematically superior to individual expert judgment — not because any individual in the collective is smarter than the expert, but because diversity of perspective enables the collective to identify solutions that no single perspective could have generated. In _Open Democracy_, she develops this argument into a case for open mini-publics and citizen assemblies chosen by lot: the randomness of selection ensures representativeness, while structured deliberation ensures that diverse perspectives are genuinely heard rather than merely present.

Taiwan's 447 citizens were not chosen because they were experts. They were chosen because they were representative. The structured deliberation they undertook — supported by AI tools designed for attentiveness — enabled them to function as a genuine cognitive system, a collective intelligence whose outputs were more reliable and more legitimate than any expert panel could have produced.

James Fishkin's Deliberative Polling methodology, developed and tested over decades in dozens of countries, demonstrates that this is not unique to Taiwan. When a random, representative sample of citizens is given balanced information and structured opportunities for small-group deliberation, their views change — consistently, reproducibly — toward greater nuance and away from the polarisation that characterises uninformed opinion. The change is not the product of persuasion or manipulation. It is the product of genuine encounter with the complexity of the issues and the legitimacy of perspectives different from one's own.

The implications for AI design are direct. AI is a powerful amplifier — what it amplifies depends on the social infrastructure in which it operates. Extractive social media, designed to maximise engagement, amplifies outrage. Deliberative civic infrastructure, designed to support collective intelligence, amplifies wisdom. The technology is not the determining variable. The social architecture in which the technology is embedded is the determining variable. And social architecture is a design choice.

### About This Book

This book was written by two people who came to civic AI from very different directions and found each other at the Oxford Institute for Ethics in AI.

Audrey Tang spent more than a decade building civic technology infrastructure in Taiwan — contributing to the g0v open government movement, developing the vTaiwan platform for public consultation, and serving as Taiwan's Digital Minister. That decade was not a series of successes. It was a decade of iteration, failure, learning, and the gradual accumulation of institutional knowledge about what it takes for digital democracy to work in practice. The deepfake deliberation of 2024 was possible because of civic infrastructure built through years of patient work that received little international attention while it was happening.

Caroline Green came to AI ethics through research on social care — the care of older people, the needs of people with complex care requirements, the institutional and relational structures through which societies provide for their most vulnerable members. Her work raised questions that the dominant frameworks for AI governance were not equipped to answer: What does it mean to design an AI system that is good for someone who is frail, isolated, cognitively impaired, or dying? How do you build accountability into systems that serve people who cannot easily advocate for themselves? What does care look like when it is mediated by technology?

The questions we ask in this book grew from the intersection of these two approaches. What would it look like if the algorithms that govern our information environment rewarded bridging across differences instead of outrage at them? What would it take to ensure that the people most affected by AI systems had genuine power to shape how those systems behave? What would it mean to make care — not efficiency, not engagement, not growth — the fundamental design principle of civic AI?

We do not have complete answers to these questions. This book is not a recipe. It is a seed — an attempt to articulate, as clearly and practically as we can, the values and principles that should guide the development of AI systems that serve democratic life. We have been genuinely collaborative in writing it, which means we have disagreed, argued, and changed each other's minds. We hope the book reflects that process: not a single voice presenting a finished position, but two perspectives in productive tension, reaching for something truer than either could have managed alone.

The inspiration of ageing and care runs through everything we have written, though it may not always be visible. Care has its meaning because of mortality — because the relationships through which we care for each other are finite, because loss is real, because the weight of what we owe each other is felt most clearly at the edges of life. AI systems do not age. They do not know loss. They do not experience the weight of finite relationships. If we want AI to serve human life — not just to process human preferences but to genuinely support the conditions under which human life flourishes — we need to design it with an honest understanding of what human life actually is.

### What to Expect

This book is a starting point and an open invitation, not a dogmatic blueprint for implementation. We have tried to write it in a way that is useful to readers approaching civic AI from very different directions: technologists who want to understand why social architecture matters as much as technical architecture; policymakers who need frameworks that go beyond risk categorisation; community organisers who are already doing this work and want language for what they are doing; and curious non-specialists who have found themselves wondering whether the AI future has to look the way it currently seems to be heading.

Chapter 2 lays the foundation by defining relational health — the quality of human connection that civic AI aims to support and that extractive AI tends to degrade. Understanding what we are trying to protect is necessary before we can evaluate whether we are protecting it. The concept of relational health gives us a measure of success that is distinct from engagement, efficiency, or capability — a measure oriented toward human flourishing rather than platform growth.

Chapters 3 through 8 introduce each element of the 6-Pack in depth, with case studies drawn from Taiwan, California, Japan, and other contexts in which civic AI tools have been developed and tested. The case studies are not cherry-picked success stories. They include failures, partial successes, and experiments whose outcomes remain uncertain. We have tried to be honest about what we know and what we do not, and to acknowledge the genuine difficulties that arise when democratic processes move at the speed of society while algorithmic systems operate at the speed of electricity.

Chapter 9 addresses the most common objections and questions that arise when civic AI frameworks are presented to audiences ranging from technologists to policymakers to community organisers. These are not rhetorical objections to be dismissed but genuine challenges to be engaged.

Chapters 10 and 11 move from principles to applications — examining how the 6-Pack can be implemented in specific domains, and offering a call to action for the communities, institutions, and individuals who want to build the civic infrastructure that democratic life requires. The call to action is addressed to all of us, not only to technologists or policymakers. Civic AI is not something that is built for citizens; it is something that citizens build, together, with the tools and processes and relationships that the 6-Pack describes.

### We, the People

"We, the people, are the superintelligence we have been waiting for."

Democracy and artificial intelligence are both technologies — both are tools for aggregating and processing distributed human intelligence into collective decisions. Neither is intrinsically good or bad. Both can be captured by concentrated power and turned against the people they were designed to serve. Both can be designed — with care, with accountability, with genuine commitment to the flourishing of the communities they operate within — to make collective life better.

The care in their symbiosis is what makes both better. Democracy without care becomes a vehicle for the tyranny of majorities; AI without care becomes a vehicle for the tyranny of objectives. The 6-Pack of Care is a practical training regimen for the civic muscles that democratic AI requires — not a technical specification to be implemented once, but a set of practices to be exercised continuously, at the speed of society, in the specific contexts of the communities we inhabit.

The Kami of Care tends the relational health of a particular place at the pace of the community that lives there. It does not optimise. It does not maximise. It does not accumulate. It cares — attentively, responsibly, competently, responsively, in solidarity, in symbiosis — and when the caring is complete, it recedes.

This is the AI we need. This is the democracy we can build. And we — plural, diverse, stubborn, caring — are the intelligence that will build it.
