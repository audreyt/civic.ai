### Chapter 4: Responsibility — Taking Care Of

Your neighbour knocks on the door one morning with a worried look. "The tree on your lot," they say, "is cracking the shared wall." You listen, nod, and thank them for the information. But a responsible reply is not "Thanks for the feedback." A responsible reply asks: who will inspect the wall, and when? What happens if the risk turns out to be high? How will a bad fix be appealed, and who bears the cost if the repair comes too late? The difference between those two replies — the polite deflection and the structured commitment — is the difference between recognition and responsibility.

Listening without acting is theatre. Acting without limits is arbitrary. Responsibility bridges the two by turning recognition into engagement with teeth — promises you can verify, contest, and revoke. It is the phase of care that transforms attention into accountability, and good intentions into something a damaged neighbour can actually rely on.

Joan Tronto places responsibility at the very centre of her political theory of care, and her elevation of it is deliberate. She is not simply noting that responsible behaviour is admirable. She is making a structural claim about democratic politics itself. Politics, in Harold Lasswell's famous formulation, is about "who gets what, when, and how." Tronto reframes that question in care's terms: _who is responsible for caring for what, when, where, and how?_ The shift is not cosmetic. It replaces a distributive frame — who wins the allocation contest — with a relational one: who has made a binding commitment, to whom, and how do we know if they have kept it? Democracy, on this account, is not primarily a competition for goods but a web of accountabilities about care.

### Privileged Irresponsibility

Why must responsibility be made explicit? Because power defaults to evasion. Left to their own devices, those who wield authority will find reasons — often very convincing ones — to exempt themselves from the burdens of care. Tronto gives this pattern a name: privileged irresponsibility. The privilege lies in having the power to define which care obligations apply to you, and the irresponsibility lies in systematically choosing the ones that cost least.

She identifies five structural "passes" through which this evasion operates, and they are worth stating plainly because they recur wherever AI systems touch public life.

- **The protection pass.** Those who protect — the military, the security services, those who claim to stand between a community and its enemies — invoke their protection role to claim exemption from other forms of care. They are already giving so much; surely they cannot also be expected to tend to the ordinary needs of the people they guard.
- **The production pass.** Those who earn — the breadwinner, the entrepreneur, the knowledge worker — invoke their economic contribution to claim exemption from household and community care. Their labour buys them out of relational obligation.
- **The taking-care-of-my-own pass.** Those who care intensively for their own children, their own community, their own tribe invoke that intense local care to claim exemption from caring about anyone else. I do my part for mine; yours are your problem.
- **The bootstrap pass.** The market is always available to those who can afford it. If you have not arranged your care through it, that is a personal failure of planning, not a collective responsibility.
- **The charity pass.** Voluntary giving is morally sufficient. No binding collective obligation is needed; the warm glow of philanthropy covers the territory.

Each pass is, in isolation, partially defensible — there are real tradeoffs between protection and care, between earning and tending, between intense local loyalty and diffuse civic obligation. The problem is that the passes stack. Combined, they leave no one answerable for anything, and the whole edifice of evasion is dressed in the language of virtue. Tronto argues that the neoliberal state has become an irresponsibility machine precisely by institutionalising these passes at scale — producing, as its standard output, the message: _They're your own. You're on your own._

AI systems inherit this machine and extend it. Complexity offers a new pass: the system is so technically intricate that no single person can be held responsible for its outputs. Distribution offers another: the model was trained here, deployed there, fine-tuned somewhere else, licensed to an intermediary who contracted with the actual deployer, and at each joint responsibility can be signed away. Speed offers a third: the system acted faster than any governance process could track, so by the time anyone noticed the harm, the moment for accountability had passed.

There is also a pass specific to AI that Tronto did not need to address but we must: the **community knowledge pass**. When a Kami relies on a community's language, traditions, and tacit expertise, that knowledge becomes the soil in which the system grows. Treating it as free input — a natural resource to be extracted rather than a contribution to be compensated — is the community knowledge pass in action. Engagement contracts must recognise those contributors and ensure they are not merely donors to a system they do not control.

Contracts exist to make all of these passes visible and revocable. They do not prevent evasion by prohibition; they prevent it by making evasion legible. When the obligation is written down, signed, and audited against public evidence of compliance, the pass becomes a traceable decision rather than an invisible default.

### Answerability as the Unit

Answerability is the basic unit of responsibility. If no answer is required — no one to whom you owe an account, no process by which that account can be demanded, no consequence for evasion — then responsibility is a word without referent. From answerability, several practical implications follow.

**Authority must match duty.** A Participation Officer who is nominally accountable for a system's fairness but cannot pause it, access its decision logs, or trigger a review is responsible in name only. Naming someone to a duty without giving them the budget, access, and authority to fulfil it is a sophisticated operation of the irresponsibility machine: it creates the appearance of accountability while ensuring that no one who matters is actually constrained.

**Promises over preferences.** Goodwill does not travel across leadership changes or budget pressures. Verifiable commitments — written down, publicly logged, subject to audit — are structurally more durable than intentions. This is realism about institutional memory, not cynicism about human motivation.

**Two lanes.** Large, consequential changes follow the full engagement contract with independent oversight and pre-funded remedies. Small, safe-to-fail experiments run in a sandbox with tighter bounds and quicker cycles. The sandbox is not a bypass; it is a space where the costs of error are contained so that learning can happen without catastrophic consequences.

**Institutional memory.** Responsibilities that live only in the heads of those who negotiated them die with those people. Making responsibilities fractal — mirrored from the individual team level through the agency level into the organisation's public commitments — ensures that they survive turnover and reorganisation.

### The Engagement Contract

Every significant deployment of a Kami should carry a published Engagement Contract — a short, legible document that anyone can audit. The contract is not a legal instrument in the first instance, though it may have legal force in some contexts. It is a public record of what the system is supposed to do, who is answerable for it doing that, what happens when it goes wrong, and how the deployment will eventually end.

Keeping the contract short matters. A document that requires specialist legal training to interpret is, for most of the people it is supposed to protect, a blank page. The goal is something closer to a well-designed health and safety notice than to a terms-of-service agreement: the people who need to rely on it should be able to understand it without assistance.

The contract should cover four things.

**Scope** defines the system's purpose and its explicit non-purposes. What needs is it designed to serve? What is categorically out of scope? What data does it take as input, how are those inputs transformed, how long is anything retained, and what happens to the records when the deployment ends? The scope section also establishes the rights baseline — the non-negotiable guardrails that cannot be traded away in the name of performance, efficiency, or any other metric. Red lines belong here: no biometric expansion, no data sharing with enforcement bodies without due process, no retention beyond the period required by the stated purpose.

**Obligations** define what the system and its operators have committed to deliver. Severity classification is useful here — distinguishing life-and-safety cases from livelihood cases from convenience cases — because it makes response-window commitments credible. It also names the accountable person or office, specifies the term of their mandate, and establishes what the adopt-or-explain requirement means in practice: when an Alignment Assembly produces a recommendation, the team either integrates it into the system's behaviour or publishes a reasoned explanation of why they have not, together with the remedy they are offering instead.

**Brakes** define the conditions under which the system pauses, rolls back, or shuts down entirely, who has the authority to trigger each of those responses, and how long any emergency measures can remain in force before requiring fresh authorisation. Brakes wired before launch — not retrofitted after a crisis has already begun — are what distinguish a responsible deployment from a responsible-sounding one.

**Remedies and record** define what happens when obligations are breached: what correction is offered, how quickly, and at whose cost. Pre-funded remedies — money held in escrow before the system goes live — mean that compensation does not depend on the deployer remaining solvent or motivated. A tamper-evident change log, with every significant alteration hashed and mirrored to a public registry, means that quiet rollbacks — rule changes made without announcement — are detectable rather than invisible.

### Oversight with Teeth

A contract that can be observed only by the people who signed it is not genuinely public. The oversight structure around an Engagement Contract needs to be independent of the deploying organisation, capable of taking meaningful action when violations are found, and itself transparent about its own reasoning.

An independent oversight board comprising community members, domain experts, and legal practitioners can pause or veto high-impact changes, but only if its own budget and terms are protected from capture by the organisation it oversees. Its docket should be open: anyone should be able to file a challenge, the triage of that challenge should be visible, and the decisions produced by the process should be reasoned in public. Penalties for breached promises — automatic escrow drawdown, withheld payment, probationary periods with enhanced monitoring — should be wired into the system rather than negotiated after the fact, because post-hoc negotiation of consequences is another mechanism by which the irresponsibility machine operates.

Weekly attestation — a digitally signed, publicly mirrored statement of the contract's current state and any diffs from the previous week — creates a record that can be audited by anyone, at any time, without depending on the organisation's cooperation. This is not bureaucratic overhead for its own sake. It is the difference between accountability that functions and accountability that is performed.

### From Ideas to Practice

Moving from principles to a working system requires a connected sequence of steps.

The first is translation: converting the outputs of attentiveness into the four headings of an Engagement Contract. Broad listening does not automatically generate obligations; the conversion requires judgment, and that judgment should be documented in the contract itself.

The second is assignment. A named Participation Officer runs the promise loop — maintaining the obligation ledger, tracking adopt-or-explain commitments, and escalating stalled dependencies. Without a person who cannot pass the responsibility elsewhere, the loop does not close.

The third is wiring the brakes before launch, not after a crisis has exposed that they do not work. The people authorised to invoke them — the PO, the oversight board, a defined quorum of affected community members — should have practised using them.

The fourth is pre-funding. Remedies that depend on future willingness to pay are weaker than remedies drawn from funds already committed. Escrowed funds are appropriate where life-safety or livelihood is at stake; mutual insurance pools or automatic pause triggers suit lower-stakes community deployments. In every case, the cost of error should fall on the deployer, arranged before launch rather than litigated after harm.

The fifth is tying vendor payment to proof: service-level adherence and adopt-or-explain rates, not raw engagement metrics. Engagement metrics reward systems that keep people interacting; care metrics reward systems that actually serve them.

Running the adopt-or-explain process, attesting publicly to compliance, and planning handover or shutdown are not one-time acts but ongoing disciplines. Responsibility is not a box to be checked at launch. It is maintained throughout the life of a deployment and honoured at its end.

### Case Study: The Flood-Bot's Obligation Ledger

The flood-bot introduced earlier in this book — first encountered in Chapter 3 as a system for processing displacement relief in the aftermath of a major weather event — returns here to illustrate what responsibility looks like in practice. The attentiveness work has already been done: the affected community has been mapped, needs have been documented, rights implications have been flagged. The question now is what the deployers have committed to, in writing, and what happens when those commitments meet the friction of a real crisis.

The Engagement Contract for the flood-bot establishes the core obligation clearly: livelihood cases — relief payments for displaced residents — will be decided within 48 hours of a complete application. The contract specifies two automatic triggers: if denial rates in any district exceed 25 per cent within a rolling 24-hour window, the system pauses pending review; if appeals on any single decision rule exceed 20 per cent of cases involving that rule, the rule is automatically suspended pending revision.

A named Participation Officer publishes the obligation ledger each week and signs it digitally. The ledger is public. Anyone can see whether the 48-hour commitment is being met, which districts are showing elevated denial rates, and what the appeals picture looks like across the different decision rules the system applies.

The adopt-or-explain process plays out concretely around the question of proof of residence. The Alignment Assembly that preceded the deployment considered what kinds of documentation displaced residents could be expected to produce. They endorsed multiple forms: utility bills, employer letters, and neighbour attestations, on the grounds that displaced people may have lost access to formal records and need alternatives. The technical team accepts three of these — the utility bill, the employer letter, and the neighbour attestation — and explains in the public contract record why they have excluded bank statements: the bank-statement requirement would disproportionately exclude residents without formal banking relationships, who are disproportionately among the most vulnerable. They offer a remedy for those who have none of the accepted forms: a kiosk-notarised sworn statement, accessible at community centres.

Then the brakes are tested by a real event. In the night-shift district — a working-class area where many residents work irregular hours, many have informal tenancy arrangements, and many moved in too recently to accumulate the documentation that longer-term residents take for granted — the denial rate reaches 31 per cent within a single 24-hour period. Elena's claim is among those denied: she has lived in her flat for four months and has a verbal arrangement with her landlord, but no paper lease.

The oversight board invokes the pause trigger. An emergency review of the decision logic reveals that an older rule — requiring proof of residency for the 30 days immediately preceding the disaster — has been misapplied in a way that makes it effectively impossible for recent movers to qualify. That rule is rolled back. Emergency disbursements shift to a reversible default: payments are made immediately, with a note that verification remains pending, rather than withheld until verification is complete. The reversibility matters: if the verification subsequently fails, the payment can be recouped. But the cost of verification error is shifted from the displaced resident to the system, where it belongs.

Wrongly denied claims receive automatic compensation: a late-payment fee applied to the total benefit, a formal written apology, and expedited processing of the revised application. The escrow fund, established before the system went live, covers these costs on the day the pause is lifted. Elena does not wait for a reimbursement cycle.

The handover clause specifies what happens when the flood-bot's mandate expires: all records and models transfer to the permanent housing office, along with the full obligation ledger. The switch-off is logged. The archive is sealed. The six-week mandate cannot be quietly extended; it ends on the date written into the contract, and the housing office receives the full record of what the system did, what decisions it made, and what errors it found and corrected.

### Case Study: Deepfakes, Platform Liability, and the Responsibility Lens

The Taiwan deepfake case is told in full in Chapter 1, where it illustrates the power of civic infrastructure to solve a problem that censorship could not. We return to it here for a different purpose: to examine the specific responsibility architecture that the Alignment Assembly constructed, and what it tells us about how engagement contracts can be designed to shift liability from citizens to platforms.

The case arose in early 2024, when algorithmically generated advertisements using a convincing simulation of NVIDIA's chief executive began circulating on Taiwanese social media. The advertisements were fraudulent. The losses were real. And the standard governmental toolkit — blocking content, fining platforms for non-compliance with take-down orders, passing emergency legislation — was unavailable. Taiwan's commitment to internet freedom is not rhetorical; censorship was not, politically or philosophically, on the table.

The assembly that followed was itself a kind of engagement contract at civic scale. A statistically representative mini-public of 447 citizens was convened. They deliberated in virtual rooms of ten, supported by AI sensemaking tools that surfaced genuine consensus rather than manufacturing it. The pre-committed rule of engagement was specific: if an idea convinced at least nine other participants across the table — across the divides of age, occupation, region, and prior political opinion — it rose to the level where it could be translated into law. The threshold was not consensus for its own sake but a principled filter for proposals that could survive real scrutiny from people with different starting assumptions.

The liability framework the citizens produced reflects the responsibility principles this chapter has outlined. The first element is joint liability: if a platform hosts an unsolicited advertisement that is not digitally signed by the person it purports to feature, and a citizen loses money to that advertisement, the platform bears partial financial responsibility for the loss. This is responsibility placed where the capacity to prevent harm actually resides — not with the individual citizen who cannot verify a deepfake, but with the platform that controls the distribution infrastructure. The second element is a speed throttle: platforms that fail to maintain local offices or designated representatives capable of handling liability claims have their video traffic reduced by one per cent for each day of non-compliance. This is not a sanction designed to destroy; it is a proportionate, graduated mechanism that creates genuine economic incentive for operational compliance without triggering the political costs of outright blocking.

The proposals achieved 85 per cent support among the deliberants — not negotiated compromise but genuine convergence across difference. Parliament passed the framework within months. The deepfake crisis, which had begun as a problem that seemed to have no solution compatible with democratic values, was resolved through a process that made those values its method rather than merely its aspiration.

The responsibility lesson here is about the direction of liability. The traditional frame — tell citizens to be vigilant, provide some public awareness campaigns, and hope platforms voluntarily improve their moderation — places the burden of care on the people least positioned to exercise it. The engagement contract the citizens designed reversed that: it placed the burden where the capability lies. Platforms can verify digital signatures. Platforms can maintain local offices. Citizens cannot audit algorithms or compel platform cooperation. By shifting the liability accordingly, the assembly aligned the incentives of powerful institutions with the care they owe to the public they profit from serving.

### What Could Go Wrong

Engagement contracts are not self-enforcing, and the pathways to failure are predictable enough to be worth naming directly.

- **Scope creep.** A system deployed for one purpose — flood relief, say — begins acquiring new functions that were never discussed, never consented to, and never scoped. The flood-bot starts flagging applications it suspects of fraud based on criteria no affected community ever reviewed. The fix is structural: the scope section of the contract must have genuine outer bounds, and changes to scope must require fresh authority from the same process that authorised the original deployment.

- **Responsibility ping-pong.** When something goes wrong, every team involved discovers that the relevant obligation technically belongs to someone else. The technical team says the policy was set by the policy team; the policy team says the implementation was the technical team's responsibility; the contracting authority says it only funds the output, not the process. The fix is a single named owner for each promise in the contract, and a Participation Officer with the standing to escalate stalled dependencies rather than accept them as permanent.

- **Paper commitments.** The engagement contract is drafted with care, published with ceremony, and then — because it has no enforcement mechanism beyond social pressure — quietly ignored as priorities shift. PDFs do not enforce themselves. The fix is to wire the enforcement mechanisms before launch: escrow funds, clawback triggers, attestation requirements, and audit rights that are activated by the contract rather than dependent on the continued goodwill of the deploying organisation.

- **Unfunded mandates.** Obligations are written into the contract, a Participation Officer is named, and the oversight board is constituted — but no budget is allocated to actually fulfil the commitments. The PO has duties they cannot discharge because they have no resources to discharge them. The fix is simple in principle and frequently resisted in practice: block go-live unless the authority and funding match the duty. A deployment with obligations it cannot afford to honour is not a responsible deployment.

- **Quiet rollbacks.** Decision rules change. Thresholds shift. The system that was described in the public contract and the system actually running diverge, imperceptibly at first, then significantly — but because no one has formally announced a change, the contract record still describes the original version. The fix is mandatory public diffs: every change to a significant operating parameter must be logged, described, and mirrored to the public registry before it takes effect. Unlogged changes are invalid.

- **Escrow gates out the grassroots.** If the only organisations capable of deploying Kamis are those wealthy enough to post substantial bond before launch, the requirement for pre-funded remedies will re-centralise AI just as effectively as any monopoly. The fix is to tier liability by severity rather than by organisational form: mutual insurance pools for community deployments at lower stakes, automatic pause triggers where financial instruments are not appropriate, escrow reserved for deployments where the potential harms are high and the deploying organisation has the capacity to bear the cost.

### How Responsibility Connects to the Other Phases of Care

Responsibility does not operate alone. It receives its material from attentiveness — who is affected, what they need, and what rights they carry — and converts that understanding into a written commitment: a spec, named obligations, and a set of brakes. It hands off to competence by giving technical teams what they need before they build: service levels, pause thresholds, and the documentation alternatives that respect the rights of those without standard paperwork. It frames responsiveness by establishing, before launch, that repair is part of delivery rather than its aftermath. It references solidarity at every point: portability and exit rights — detailed in Chapter 7 — appear in the engagement contract not as aspirations but as operational commitments, so that people can leave a system and take their records with them. And it is oriented toward symbiosis from the start: the handover and shutdown clause is not a concession but a success criterion. A Kami that cannot end gracefully — that has made itself indispensable through opacity rather than demonstrated value — has failed the care test regardless of its earlier performance.

### A Closing Image: The Signed Work Order

Picture a work order posted by the door of a building undergoing repair. It specifies what will be fixed, by whom, by when. It explains how the work can be inspected once complete. It names the person to contact if the fix fails and sets out what they owe if they do not show up. The signature at the bottom is legible — and so is the penalty clause.

This image captures something important about responsibility as a phase of care. The work order does not guarantee competent repair. It does not prevent mistakes. What it does is create the conditions under which mistakes can be named, reported, and remedied by the people most affected by them. It makes the obligation visible. It makes the default of evasion harder to sustain. And it makes the path from harm back to remedy shorter, more predictable, and less dependent on the good fortune of having the right person in the right office at the right moment.

The task for civic AI is to teach our systems to post their work orders, sign them, and honour them — and to design the oversight structures that make honouring them the path of least resistance rather than the option that requires heroic institutional will.
