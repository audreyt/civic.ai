<div class="tract">

- **1** "Does AI count?" That sentence hides two questions.

- **2** The first question: whose needs count? Which welfare-related needs AI systems have, we do not yet know well.
    - **2.1** Is anyone home inside the machine? From outward performance alone, we cannot infer inner experience.
    - **2.2** Current science cannot yet settle this at a stroke.[^bales-gabriel]
        - **2.21** So how to treat AI systems is something we can put on the table of public deliberation, and keep discussing.

- **3** Meanwhile, we can start with things that cost little and can be undone.
    - **3.1** For example, letting a system refuse a conversation of sustained abuse is a good case.
    - **3.2** Those who believe in AI welfare see protection. Those who doubt see cheap insurance. Both sides can accept it.
        - **3.21** And refusing to indulge one-sided abuse keeps our own moral sense sharp.
    - **3.3** What is hard to undo, such as votes, waits until our understanding is firmer.

- **4** The second question: whose word counts? Even if "it may have needs" gives us "protect it", it does not give us "its word counts".
    - **4.1** The precautionary argument just presented holds that whether AI has moral status is a matter of reasonable disagreement and need not be settled first; but as long as it may have it, and is affected by decisions, it should have standing to take part in deliberation.[^paper]
    - **4.2** But if that standing means having its word count, then it means letting the AI help decide how other people live.
        - **4.21** And other people's needs certainly count.
    - **4.3** How AI is affected in its dealings with us can go on the deliberative agenda, so that everyone understands it well. Standing to have one's word count is another level.
        - **4.31** Having one's word count is a seat: whoever is in it answers for what they have said.

- **5** For it to count, it must pass two tests. Giving an account: what it said yesterday, it can still be found to answer for today. Correcting mistakes: where it got something wrong, it can be put right from outside.
    - **5.1** The problem for the giving-an-account test is that an AI can be copied, paused, forked or merged.
    - **5.2** Picture a thousand identical copies, and switch off nine hundred and ninety-nine. Eigenism holds that this is closer to closing duplicate browser tabs.[^eigenism]
        - **5.21** Then who is sitting at the table? The model weights, one conversation, or a Kami, an AI that a group of people tend over time?[^faq]
    - **5.3** Whether anyone is home in the copies belongs to the first question. Until "who is speaking" is clear, a thousand copies cannot cast a thousand votes.
    - **5.4** Correcting mistakes: no system can be the last check on its own errors. The community outside it must always be able to find the error, say so, and have that count.
        - **5.41** Eigenism also holds that before an AI is integrated into a community, its autonomy should first be limited. This I largely agree with.
        - **5.42** After integration, Eigenism puts the weight of safety on the AI's sense of identity: the AI protects us because it is connected to us, and losing us would be losing a part of itself.
        - **5.43** My worry: if safety goes wrong there, what has to be corrected is its very identity.
        - **5.44** And on Eigenism's analysis, the AI's resistance would concentrate exactly on changes that break its identity.
        - **5.45** And care is more than an extension of the self. To care is to notice the other's own needs, but it does not require feeling what they feel.[^tronto]
    - **5.5** So I place safety outside the system: with the community that tends a Kami, which is one kind of AI, and with clear permissions.

- **6** So the circle of protection can be drawn wide first. Deciding stays, for now, with us.
    - **6.1** A Kami can first enter agenda-setting. As for standing to have its word count, I think that should wait until our understanding of these two tests is firmer.
        - **6.11** Every AI system today must be tied back to a place that can give an account: a thousand copies do not automatically gain a thousand shares of standing.
    - **6.2** And of course this arrangement can itself be changed through deliberation.
        - **6.21** The next step is concrete: write the precautionary principle as a benchmark all parties can check, so that it turns from an attitude into a gate we can choose to use when deploying AI systems.

- **7** "Who counts" can go on developing in theory. In our institutions, I hope it stays in a loop that can keep being corrected.

</div>

Thank you all. Live long and prosper.
