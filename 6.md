A clinic posts hours, yet patients show up to a locked door. A responsive clinic apologises, posts why it happened, updates hours, and texts people next time. The fix becomes part of the system.

Competent action creates new information. The person who was served — or who was failed — now knows something about the system that the system does not yet know about itself. Refusing to receive that knowledge is the fastest path to repeated failure. Joan Tronto defines responsiveness as "observing that response and making judgements about it — whether the care given was sufficient, successful, or complete." And she adds a point that is easy to overlook: "the person cared for need not be the one who responds" — others in the care setting can assess. Those best positioned to judge whether care worked are often not the engineers who built it but the people who live closest to its effects.

For Civic AI, this reformulation of the public question is decisive. Chapter 5 checked whether the process ran as promised. Chapter 6 checks whether the care actually landed. The shift is not cosmetic. It changes who holds the author's pen for evaluation, and it changes what counts as repair.

### Why Responsiveness Begins with Community Judgement

There is a temptation, particularly in organisations with strong engineering cultures, to treat feedback as an input to a technical pipeline: collect ratings, aggregate scores, retrain the model, ship an improved version. This pattern is not wrong, but it is not sufficient, and when it is treated as sufficient it produces a specific kind of failure.

Traditional responsiveness in public services relies on aggregate, top-down, quantitative metrics. Net promoter scores. Customer satisfaction surveys. SurveyMonkey forms sent to everyone who interacted with a service in the past month. These instruments are not useless — they can detect large-scale shifts in sentiment — but they are structurally blind to the knowledge that lives at the edge of the system, closest to the people the system is supposed to serve.

Consider a concrete case: a bedside nursing AI deployment in a hospital in Pingtung. The system monitors patient vitals and suggests interventions. The aggregate satisfaction scores for the ward are good. Response times are within targets. The post-deployment survey shows that nurses feel the system is helpful. None of this captures what Nurse Wei — who has worked the overnight shift on this ward for eleven years — noticed at three in the morning last Tuesday.

The patient in bed seven had a breathing pattern that the monitoring system's alerts did not flag. The numbers were in range. But Nurse Wei has heard this patient's overnight breathing for three weeks, and the texture of the sound had changed — not dramatically, not in a way that would trigger any threshold, but in the way that you hear a change in a person you have listened to carefully for a long time. She adjusted his position, checked his oxygen saturation manually, and called the doctor. The patient had been developing a slow pneumothorax that would have become a crisis by morning.

No SurveyMonkey form captures the tacit feedback that Nurse Wei's eleven years of night-shift _mohui_ — the embodied knowledge carried from Chapter 5's Public Master model — made available that night. The monitoring system did not notice what she noticed. More importantly, she cannot fully articulate what she noticed; it lives in the accumulated sensory memory of hundreds of nights, not in any description she could write in a feedback box.

This is not an argument against quantitative monitoring. It is an argument that quantitative monitoring, however well-designed, captures a different kind of knowledge from the situated, qualitative, relational knowledge that the people closest to care accumulate over time. Responsiveness, in the full sense that Tronto intends, requires both.

### The Brake as a Care Mechanism

Nurse Wei's ability to act on what she noticed — to override the monitoring system's implicit recommendation that nothing needed to be done — depends on a specific structural property of the care system: the existence of a reliable, accessible override mechanism. Call it, following the language of Chapter 5, the brake.

In the Public Master model, the brake is the mechanism that lets the human operator halt the AI when it drifts or errs. In the context of responsiveness, the brake appears at a different level: it is the mechanism by which the care-receiver — or the care-giver on their behalf — can say "this time, the automated model is wrong; stop, let me override, or route this to a human immediately." Without this brake, feedback loops cannot land. They can generate signals; they can change future training distributions; but they cannot interrupt a current wrong decision in the moment when interruption is what care requires.

This is why Tronto's insistence that responsiveness must involve the possibility of actual change is so important. A reply that cannot cause change is, as she would put it, theatre. It is the performance of listening without the reality of it. A Civic AI system that accepts feedback through a designated channel, processes it through a designated pipeline, and produces improvements on a designated schedule is performing responsiveness. A system that allows the affected person — or the professional closest to them — to stop a specific action now, on the grounds that it is wrong for this person in this situation, is practising it.

The implementation of the brake at the responsiveness level varies by context. For Nurse Wei, it means she can override the monitoring system's non-alert and call the doctor without having to justify the override to a software interface. For a resident appealing a flood-relief decision, it means there is a single accessible action — a button, a call, a form that takes under two minutes — that initiates a human review of her specific case. For a language community whose members have been systematically misclassified by an AI system that doesn't adequately handle their dialect, it means there is a mechanism by which the community can flag the pattern and trigger a policy review, not merely lodge individual complaints.

In all these cases, what makes the brake functional is not the sophistication of the mechanism but its accessibility and its consequentiality. It must be reachable without navigating a maze. And it must actually stop something.

### Community-Authored Evaluation

The care-receiving phase of the cycle introduces a question that the care-giving phase cannot answer from the inside: did it work? The engineers who built the system cannot be the primary authors of the answer, for the same reason that a surgeon should not self-assess whether a patient recovered. The person who received the care — and, more broadly, the community that understands the context in which care was delivered — must hold the author's pen.

This principle has practical implications for how evaluation infrastructure is built. Rather than a regime in which labs design benchmarks that their own systems are trained to perform well on, we propose what might be called a Wikipedia model for evaluations: a shared, living registry of tests that anyone can draft, that civil society partners can review and curate, and that AI developers are expected to adopt or explain why they have not. The key features are community authorship, public visibility, and the separation of those who design the tests from those whose systems are being tested.

Community-authored evaluations carry a form of knowledge that lab-designed benchmarks systematically underrepresent. When a language community affected by a flood-relief AI writes an evaluation of translation fidelity, they are encoding knowledge about their dialect, their idioms, the specific ways that bureaucratic language translates poorly into community understanding. When nursing staff on a particular ward write an evaluation of a bedside monitoring AI, they are encoding the _mohui_ of that ward — the baseline rhythms, the characteristic presentations of the patient population, the situational factors that affect what normal looks like at three in the morning. This knowledge is not available to the lab. It cannot be purchased or scraped. It is produced by proximity and time.

There is a labour question here that must be addressed directly. The work of evaluation — identifying harms, articulating what went wrong, designing tests that will catch similar failures in the future, reviewing other community members' submissions, moderating the registry — is real labour. It takes time, expertise, and sustained attention. If that labour is expected to be provided for free, as a kind of civic contribution, the evaluation process will structurally under-represent the communities with the least time and the most precarity, which are often the communities whose engagement matters most. The Engagement Contract from Chapter 4 must include provision for compensating this work. Community knowledge is labour, not free quality assurance.

### The Appeal as Care Infrastructure

The right to appeal is the formal structure through which care-receiving is institutionalised. But the word "appeal" can mislead: it sounds like a legal process, something reserved for serious grievances and formal contestation. The model we advocate is closer to the nurse noticing the change in the patient's breathing — an ordinary, low-friction mechanism for saying "this doesn't seem right, let me check."

Every decision that a Civic AI system makes should show a one-click route to challenge it, with a visible clock indicating when a response can be expected. Urgent cases — where a decision is causing ongoing harm — warrant a 48-hour response. Standard cases warrant seven days. Complex cases involving policy questions or systemic issues warrant thirty days. These timelines are not aspirational; they are commitments in the Engagement Contract, with consequences for breach.

The appeals process is also the primary mechanism through which individual experience is converted into system-level learning. A single appeal from a single affected person identifies a potential failure. A cluster of appeals on a similar issue — say, a wave of translation complaints from a specific language community, as in the flood-bot case — identifies a systemic one. The process of converting that cluster into a community-authored evaluation, and that evaluation into a release gate for future model updates, is how the care cycle closes.

What makes this work is not the technical pipeline — though the pipeline matters — but the cultural commitment that underlies it. Blameless post-mortems, a practice borrowed from operational engineering and applied here to the governance of AI systems, examine failures at the level of mechanism and system design rather than individual fault. The goal is not to identify who did something wrong but to understand what structural features of the system made the failure possible, and to change those features so that the failure cannot recur. The incident page that documents a failure — what happened, whom it affected, what changed, which new test now guards against future instances — is the public record of that commitment. It is not a wall of shame; it is, as our closing image suggests, a wall of learning.

### Closing the Loop with Elena

The flood-bot case that ran through Chapters 4 and 5 reaches its responsiveness phase with a surge of appeals from a particular language community. Bilingual residents have been receiving denial notices that, when read carefully, cite requirements that were translated in ways that do not match the actual requirements. The bureaucratic Mandarin phrasing for "a signed statement from a neighbour" was rendered in ways that residents understood as requiring something more — a notarised document, perhaps, or something stamped by a local official.

Community partners bring this to the oversight board's attention. Rather than treating it as an isolated translation error, they propose a community-authored evaluation: a set of test cases that probe the bot's handling of translated documentation requirements across the range of phrasing the community actually uses. The partners are compensated from the project's escrow fund — their time spent designing the test cases, reviewing the bot's outputs, and revising the evaluation based on initial findings is explicitly labour, carried in the Engagement Contract as a line item alongside infrastructure costs.

The bot fails the evaluation. The pause trigger fires. Reversible defaults apply to all pending cases in the affected language community: rather than auto-denial, cases are routed to bilingual human review. The pause is not indefinite; the Engagement Contract specifies that the system cannot re-deploy to this population until it passes the community-authored evaluation.

The fix takes nine days. Bilingual reviewers work through the documentation requirements, produce plain-language renderings that have been tested against the community evaluation, and update the bot's policy rules. The new rules pass the evaluation. The evaluation is added to the eval registry as a permanent release gate: no future update to the flood-bot's documentation requirements can be deployed without passing this test.

Elena — whose case was among those paused — receives a text message. It does not say "we apologise for the inconvenience." It says: "We found an error in how our system translated the documentation requirements you received. We fixed the error. Here is your new decision. Here is how to see what changed. If you have questions about your case, here is how to reach a caseworker directly." The decision is favourable. The link to the change log shows, in plain language, what rule changed and why.

Trust-under-loss — the measure of whether people who experienced a bad outcome and attempted repair judge the process as fair enough to accept — ticks upward. Not because Elena got the outcome she wanted, though that matters. But because the system did what it said it would do when things went wrong, and because she can see that it did.

### What Could Go Wrong

Responsiveness, like competence, fails in characteristic ways.

**The appeal maze** — too many steps, unclear status, no visible clock — defeats the purpose of having an appeals process at all. People abandon appeals not because they accept the decision but because the effort required exceeds what they can sustain. The fix is structural: a single button, auto-escalation if the SLA is breached without response, and a status visible to the appellant at every stage.

**Eval spam** — a flood of low-quality or malicious test submissions overwhelming the community review process — is the mirror image of the appeal maze. Where the maze puts too much friction between citizens and the system, eval spam puts too little. The fix is partner moderation with published standards, reputation-based weighting for contributors, and merge/duplicate detection to consolidate overlapping submissions. None of these mechanisms should be so restrictive that they privilege established civil society organisations over community members with direct experience; the goal is quality, not credentialing.

**Blame storms** — post-incident processes that focus on identifying individual fault rather than systemic cause — destroy the culture of candour that blameless post-mortems require. When people fear being identified as responsible for a failure, they become reluctant to report near-misses, to flag emerging patterns, or to raise concerns before something goes wrong. The fix is a consistent institutional commitment to blameless process, not as a slogan but as a practice enforced from senior leadership down.

**Weaponised appeals** — adversarial actors deliberately flooding the appeals system, or strategically triggering pause mechanisms to disrupt service — present a harder challenge, because the same mechanisms that make appeals accessible to affected communities make them accessible to bad-faith actors. The fix requires authenticated standing: not public identity, which would chill legitimate complaints from people who fear retaliation, but some form of verified connection to the affected population. Rate-limiting by community prevents individual actors from consuming disproportionate capacity. Priority access for those directly affected by a specific decision must be preserved even as aggregate volume is managed.

### The Cycle Restarts

Tronto observes that "new needs will undoubtedly arise" — that responsiveness does not close the care cycle so much as return it to its beginning. The attentiveness that Chapter 3 described as the first phase of care is shaped by everything that responsiveness reveals. The needs that the community surfaces through appeals, through community-authored evaluations, through the patterns visible in the incident registry, are new inputs to attentiveness: new things to notice, new populations whose situation was not fully visible before.

This is not a weakness of the framework; it is its essential character. Care is not a pipeline with a terminus. It is a cycle that sustains itself by learning. The system that hides its breaks — that treats the wall of retired broken parts as a wall of shame — will repeat them. The system that memorialises its failures, compensates the labour of those who identified them, and makes the resulting tests a permanent part of its release infrastructure has built something that is capable of becoming, over time, genuinely trustworthy.

The public measure for this chapter is trust-under-loss: after a bad outcome and attempted repair, do affected people report that the system became more trustworthy rather than less? A supporting diagnostic is whether people who lost on the merits still judge the process and its repair as fair enough to accept. These are not soft measures. They are the indicators of whether a Civic AI system is doing the thing that distinguishes it from a system that merely performs accountability — whether it is actually responsive to the people it is meant to serve.

### The Workshop Wall

The workshop with a wall of retired broken parts — each tagged with the story of how it broke, how to avoid future damage, and who fixed it — is the image we hold for responsiveness. That wall is public. It is not curated to show only the recoveries; it shows the breaks. It is not organised to minimise the appearance of failure; it is organised to maximise the extraction of learning from each failure. And it is maintained by the community that uses the workshop, not only by the engineers who built the machines.

A Civic AI system that aspires to responsiveness in Tronto's full sense is building that wall, in public, every time something goes wrong and every time the community speaks. The wall is the proof that the care cycle is real — that the listening leads to change, that the change is visible, and that the next iteration of the system carries, encoded in its evaluation registry and its release gates, the accumulated knowledge of everyone who has been failed and spoken up about it.

The shop that hides its breaks will repeat them. The shop that builds the wall becomes, over time, the most reliable shop in the district — not because it never breaks anything, but because it has made its failures the foundation of its competence.
