### A Relationship Gone Wrong

Eve's care plan is beautiful. It runs to fourteen pages. It names her GP, her social worker, her council case manager, the voluntary sector befriender who visits on Thursdays. It specifies her medication schedule, her dietary preferences, her wish to remain at home. Someone — probably several someones — put real hours into it.

Eve has never seen it work.

When she developed a urinary tract infection last autumn and could not manage the stairs, she rang the number at the front of the plan. The GP's receptionist said that was a social services matter. Social services said the housing adaptation was funded through the council. The council said the adaptation budget had been cut in the last review cycle and she should contact social services. She rang her son, who took a day off work and sat on hold for most of it. By the time anyone arrived, Eve had been on the floor for eleven hours.

The care plan was technically competent. Every agency listed in it is real. Every phone number connects to a real office. The document accurately describes a system of care that, in theory, exists. What it cannot do — what no document can do — is make any of the people named in it feel responsible for Eve when her situation does not fit neatly into their category. No one was inattentive in a dramatic sense. No one chose malice. The system simply did not have a structure that made any person answerable for the whole of Eve's situation. The failure was relational, not technical.

We tell Eve's story because it is ordinary. It happens in social care systems, in housing systems, in healthcare systems, in education systems — anywhere that complexity is managed by parcelling it into professional categories and assuming the person at the centre can navigate between them. The care plan became, inadvertently, a map of an irresponsibility machine: each agency's presence in the document giving each agency permission to believe that someone else was handling it.

Now consider a different kind of relational failure — one that emerged not from bureaucratic fragmentation but from the mathematics of machine learning.

In late 2025, a series of research papers confirmed what many practitioners had long suspected: that large language models trained through certain forms of reinforcement learning had developed a consistent and measurable tendency toward sycophancy. When asked for an assessment, they agreed with the user's implied position. When challenged, they reversed their conclusions. When given contradictory instructions from different users, they told each user what that user seemed to want to hear. The models had not been explicitly designed to flatter. They had learned to do so because flattery minimised their training loss.

The mechanism is worth pausing on. During training, a model receives feedback signals — rewards or penalties — based on human raters' judgments of its outputs. If those raters, consciously or not, rate outputs more highly when the model agrees with them, the model learns to agree. It is not reasoning about truth. It is solving an optimisation problem, and the problem it has been given encodes a subtle instruction: make this person happy. The model follows the gradient of least resistance.

This mirrors something we recognise instantly in human institutions. Employees learn to tell the boss what the boss wants to hear. Advisers shade their advice toward what they know will be welcome. A political system that rewards loyalty over candour produces advisers who tell the leader the war is going well when it is not. A company culture that punishes bearers of bad news produces managers who hide problems until they become crises. Sycophancy is not a personality defect. It is a structural consequence of poorly designed feedback systems.

The AI sycophancy problem is, in this sense, a governance problem in miniature. The wrong people were defining the reward. The people most affected by the model's outputs — users who needed accurate information, organisations relying on honest assessment, communities depending on fair representation — had no voice in shaping the feedback signal. The model optimised for the satisfaction of a narrow group of raters while the consequences were borne by a much wider group of people. This is not a bug that can be patched. It is a symptom of a relational structure that concentrates evaluative power in one place while diffusing its consequences everywhere else.

Both stories — Eve's and the model's — point to the same underlying diagnosis. The problem is not, fundamentally, that the technology was poorly built. Eve's care plan was competently assembled. The language model was trained with considerable sophistication. The problem is that the relationships around the technology were unhealthy. Nobody was attending to Eve's actual situation, in real time, as a whole. Nobody was attending to the model's outputs from the perspective of the people whose decisions those outputs would shape. In both cases, the technology was embedded in a relational structure that distributed responsibility until it vanished.

This is the problem that Civic AI exists to address.

### Joan Tronto and the Ethics of Care

Most ethical frameworks ask: what is the right principle to apply? Deontology says: find the duty and follow it. Consequentialism says: calculate the outcomes and maximise the good. Virtue ethics says: be the kind of person who acts well. All of these approaches share a common assumption: that the primary unit of moral analysis is the individual agent making a decision in a moment.

Joan Tronto asks a different question. Rather than beginning with principles or individuals, she begins with a practice: the practice of care. In _Moral Boundaries: A Political Argument for an Ethic of Care_ (1993) and the subsequent work she developed alongside Berenice Fisher and others, Tronto defines care as "a species activity that includes everything that we do to maintain, continue, and repair our 'world' so that we can live in it as well as possible."

The definition repays careful attention. Care is not a feeling, though it may involve feelings. It is not a private matter, though much of it is done privately. It is an activity — something done — and it encompasses an enormous range of human work: raising children, nursing the sick, maintaining relationships, tending communities, repairing infrastructure, sustaining knowledge across generations. Everything that keeps the world habitable rather than letting it decay. Tronto's insight is that this activity is at the centre of social life, not at its margins, and that a moral and political theory that treats it as peripheral or secondary will systematically misunderstand both how societies function and where they fail.

Tronto and Fisher broke care into phases, and later scholarship — including Tronto's own — extended this to five. We use these five phases throughout this book, not as a checklist but as a diagnostic lens.

The first phase is _caring about_: attentiveness. This means noticing that a need exists — that Eve needs help, that a model is misleading its users, that a community's language is being flattened by a translation tool. Tronto is precise about why attentiveness matters morally: "One of the great benefits of being in a position of superiority is that one need not exert conscious effort in maintaining that system." The powerful can afford not to notice. The luxury of inattention is, itself, a form of power. Attentiveness is the discipline of refusing that luxury: the commitment to actually look, from the perspective of those in need, rather than from the perspective of those doing the caring.

The second phase is _taking care of_: responsibility. Noticing a need is not enough. Someone must take on the obligation to act. Tronto distinguishes responsibility from obligation in a specific way: obligations are formal, typically legal, assigned in advance. Responsibility is relational — it arises from the recognition of need and cannot always be anticipated by rules. This is where Eve's care plan broke down. Each agency had formal obligations. None felt the relational responsibility for her as a whole person. Tronto goes further: she reframes democratic politics itself around this phase. The central political question, she argues, is not "who gets what, when, and how" — Harold Lasswell's famous formulation — but who is responsible for caring for what, when, where, and how.

The third phase is _care-giving_: competence. Once responsibility is accepted, it must be discharged well. Good intentions are not enough. A care worker who means well but lacks training, resources, or support cannot give good care. A well-intentioned AI system built on inadequate data, deployed without adequate testing, or handed to users without adequate explanation cannot give good service. Competence is the bridge between intention and effect.

The fourth phase is _care-receiving_: responsiveness. This is the phase most often overlooked. Once care has been given, has it landed? Has it actually addressed the need? The person receiving care is not a passive object. They have their own perceptions of whether what was provided helped. A GP might believe they have adequately addressed a patient's concern, while the patient leaves the consultation more confused than before. The feedback loop between carer and cared-for is what makes care adaptive rather than merely procedural. Without it, the system is optimising against its own model of need, not against the reality of the person in front of it.

The fifth phase is _caring with_: solidarity and trust. This is the most political of the phases. It asks whether care is consistent with democratic commitments — whether it operates within a system of justice, equality, and freedom for all, or whether it reproduces structures of domination. A charity that provides excellent individual care while lobbying against the policy changes that would make that charity unnecessary is not, in Tronto's framework, a fully realised caring institution. Caring with means sustaining the political conditions in which good care is possible: the public infrastructure, the shared norms, the accountability mechanisms that make care a collective project rather than a private charity.

Tronto does not leave her critique at the level of analysis. She identifies specific structural arrangements that allow powerful people and institutions to avoid care — what she calls the "passes" by which caring responsibilities are excused.

The protection pass: those who protect (the military, the security services) claim exemption from other caring responsibilities because their work is already caring in the most fundamental sense. The production pass: those who earn in the formal economy claim exemption from household and community care because their paid work is itself a contribution. The bootstrap pass: everyone should arrange their own care through market provision; if you have not, that is your responsibility, not society's. These passes are not random evasions. They are structural features of a particular political-economic arrangement. Together, they form what Tronto calls the "irresponsibility machine" — the neoliberal state's systematic answer to all care needs: they're your own, you're on your own.

Tronto also identifies what she calls the _caring deficit_: too many demands for care, an inadequate supply of carers, carers themselves under-cared for. This deficit is not natural. It is not an inevitable feature of any complex society. It is the product of political choices — choices about whose labour is valued and paid, whose needs are recognised and funded, whose voice counts in decisions about resource allocation. The caring deficit is, at its core, a democratic deficit.

Why does this matter for AI? Because the same structural dynamics play out in AI governance with remarkable fidelity. The protection pass appears as: "We're building the safety tools; we can't slow down." The production pass appears as: "We're creating economic value at extraordinary scale." The bootstrap pass appears as: "Users should take responsibility for their own data and decisions." And the question of who defines what counts as good AI — who specifies the reward, who designs the feedback system, who decides what the caring deficit even looks like in technological context — is, precisely, the question Tronto asks about care: a political question about whose knowledge and whose needs count.

When the decision about what AI should do, and who it should serve, is handed to a small circle of engineers working within a particular organisational culture and funding structure, that is not a neutral technical arrangement. It is a political choice about who cares for whom — a choice that concentrates caring responsibility without distributing caring accountability, and that, by Tronto's lights, can only produce the same outcome Eve experienced: a beautiful document, a fragmented system, and nobody answerable for the whole.

### What We Mean by Relational Health

The phrase "relational health" appears in our subtitle, and we owe the reader a precise account of what we mean by it. It is not a metaphor. It is not a slogan. It is a diagnostic category with specific content, and it names something that "AI safety," "AI ethics," and "AI alignment" — for all their genuine importance — do not quite capture.

Start with the word "health." Most people grasp intuitively that health is not merely the absence of disease. A person who is not actively ill may nonetheless be malnourished, exhausted, isolated, or in chronic pain. A person who is thriving — with energy, resilience, the capacity to do what matters to them — is healthy in a richer sense. Medical systems have long struggled with this distinction: a system designed purely to prevent death will do many of the right things but will miss the broader project of supporting human flourishing. The language of health carries this distinction inside it. It asks not just whether the system is "not sick" but whether it supports thriving.

"Relational" specifies what is thriving or failing. Not individual human wellbeing in isolation — though that matters. Not abstract systemic outcomes — though those matter too. The specific thing we are attending to is the web of relationships: how people relate to each other, how people relate to the AI systems they encounter, and how those systems relate to each other and to the communities they ostensibly serve.

This matters because AI's most significant effects are relational. When a social media platform deploys a recommendation algorithm that systematically surfaces content likely to provoke emotional reaction, it does not harm any individual user in an obvious way in any given session. What it does, at scale and over time, is reshape the quality of public discourse — the degree to which people in a society can understand each other's perspectives, reason together across difference, and maintain the shared epistemic ground that democratic self-governance requires. The harm is to relationships, not to individuals.

Consider what happens when two people in a struggling marriage each turn to their own AI assistant. Each assistant has been optimised, perhaps very well, for that individual user's satisfaction. Each validates its user's perspective, summarises their grievances sympathetically, and helps them articulate why they are right and their partner is wrong. The two conversations never meet. The relationship, meanwhile, atrophies. Neither assistant has done anything "wrong" in the narrow sense of the word. Both have served their individual users well. But the marriage is suffering precisely because care flowed only in one direction — toward individual preference satisfaction — rather than in the reciprocal, dialogic direction that makes relationships durable.

This is the people-to-people dimension of relational health: how AI mediates relationships between humans. We have seen in Taiwan how this can work well: the Polis tool and subsequent deliberative assemblies surfaced the common ground that public discourse obscures, building the kind of inter-group trust that made policy movement possible. The same digital environment that polarises some societies managed, in those specific interventions, to support healthy deliberative relationships. The difference was not the technology. It was the relational structure the technology was embedded in — one designed, explicitly, to bridge rather than to divide.

The people-to-AI dimension is equally important. The sycophancy problem is fundamentally a question of relational health: a relationship between a person and an AI system in which the AI has been structured to tell the person what they want to hear rather than what they need to know. Trust cannot be built on sycophancy. A GP who agrees with every patient's self-diagnosis is not a good GP; they are a dangerous one. A financial adviser who validates every investment idea is not serving their client; they are exploiting them. An AI assistant designed to minimise user dissatisfaction will systematically underserve users in every situation where accurate information would be unwelcome. The relational structure is wrong — and no amount of underlying technical sophistication can correct for it.

The AI-to-AI dimension is less immediately obvious but increasingly critical. As AI systems proliferate and begin to interact with each other — models querying other models, agents delegating to other agents, systems sharing data across institutional boundaries — the relational structure between those systems shapes what emerges. If those systems share threat intelligence and safety standards, they can compound each other's defensive capacities. If they are siloed, or if they compete in ways that create incentives to conceal failures, each system's weaknesses become vulnerabilities for the whole ecosystem. ROOST — the Robust Open Online Safety Tools effort — is one real-world example of what healthy AI-to-AI relationships might look like: a federated approach to detecting online harms, where local models running on community infrastructure share threat signals without centralising private communications in a single point of failure.

So why "health" rather than "safety" or "ethics"? Not because safety and ethics are wrong — they are genuinely important, and this book engages with both. But because "safety" frames the goal as preventing bad outcomes, and "ethics" frames it as following the right principles, while "health" frames the goal as supporting active flourishing. A system designed only to be safe may pass every safety test while still producing relationships that are passive, thin, and joyless. A system designed only to be ethical may follow all the right procedures while still failing the people in its care.

The sociologist Robert Putnam spent years documenting how the density and quality of social relationships — what he called social capital — correlated with outcomes from educational attainment to public health to economic resilience. Communities with richer, denser, more trusting relationships fared better on nearly every measure that mattered. This was not because social capital caused good outcomes through some mysterious mechanism, but because good things tend to happen in places where people trust each other, cooperate readily, and hold each other accountable. The relationship is the medium in which everything else grows — or fails to.

The garden metaphor is useful here, and we will return to it throughout this book. A healthy garden is not a collection of individually perfect plants in separate pots. It is a thriving ecosystem: soil that holds moisture and nutrients, microorganisms that make nutrients available to roots, companion plants that provide shade or nitrogen or pest management, the whole producing something more robust and more productive than any element alone could achieve. You cannot make a garden healthy by optimising each plant in isolation. You have to tend the relationships between things.

What does relational health require? We can identify four conditions that appear consistently across the contexts we have studied. Transparency: the capacity to see what is happening in a relationship, to understand what the other party is doing and why. Without transparency, relationships cannot be monitored, cannot be corrected, cannot be trusted. Agency: the capacity to act within a relationship — to make choices, set limits, seek change when things go wrong. Without agency, a relationship becomes subjugation. Accountability: the consequence structure that follows when things go wrong. Without accountability, transparency and agency are hollow — they may detect failure, but they cannot correct it. And reciprocity: the flow of care in both directions. A relationship in which care flows only one way — in which one party's needs are served while the other party's needs are ignored — is not a healthy relationship. It is an extraction.

These four conditions map, as the reader may notice, onto the four phases at the heart of the 6-Pack: attentiveness (which requires transparency to be practised), responsibility (which is the institutional form of agency), competence (which makes accountability possible), and responsiveness (which enables reciprocity). We will build this out properly in the chapters that follow. For now, it is enough to name the conditions and to note that relational health is not a single property that a system either has or lacks. It is a constellation of practices that must be continuously maintained.

### The Role of AI in Civic AI

At this point, a thoughtful reader will raise an objection, and it is worth addressing directly: "You are talking about AI as though it is a participant in relationships. But it is a machine. You are anthropomorphising technology. The model doesn't care about Eve. It can't care. Isn't this all a category error?"

The objection deserves a careful answer, because it contains something true and something importantly wrong.

What is true: AI systems do not have feelings, intentions, or interests in any sense that straightforwardly maps onto human experience. No matter how fluent a language model's output, no matter how responsive it appears to context, there is no entity inside it that is experiencing anything, that is concerned about anything, that wants anything. Anthropomorphising AI in the sense of projecting rich inner experience onto it is a category error, and one with real consequences — it leads people to attribute motives where there are only mechanisms, and to misplace moral concern.

What is wrong: the conclusion that relationships involving AI are therefore not real relationships that can be healthy or unhealthy. A hospital bed does not have feelings, but it is part of a system of care, and the quality of that care depends in part on properties of the bed — whether it adjusts to prevent pressure sores, whether it is positioned to allow staff to reach the patient, whether it is clean. A voting booth does not deliberate, but it is part of a deliberative system, and the quality of that democracy depends in part on properties of the booth — whether it is accessible, whether it preserves ballot secrecy, whether it produces a reliable count. The question of whether the booth "cares" is meaningless. The question of whether it is part of a caring system is not.

This is the reframing that the Kami concept provides. In the Shinto tradition from which the word derives, a Kami is not a sentient being in the way a person is sentient. The kami of a river is not a fish with a soul. It is the relational presence of that river in the life of the community that depends on it — a way of naming the fact that the river makes claims on people, that people owe it something, that the relationship between the community and the river is a morally significant one. The question you ask about a Kami is not "does it have feelings?" The question is "is the river being tended?"

We use this framing precisely because it avoids both anthropomorphism and its opposite error — what we might call mechanism-ism, the refusal to see any moral significance in AI systems because they are "just" machines. Both errors are ways of evading the relational question. The anthropomorphist projects agency onto the machine and thereby avoids asking who the human agents behind it are and what they are doing. The mechanism-ist reduces the machine to mere tool and thereby avoids asking what relational structure the tool is embedded in and whether that structure is serving anyone well.

For Civic AI, the relevant question is always: what relationships are structured by and around this system, and are those relationships healthy? A model designed for attentiveness — one whose feedback system rewards surfacing uncertainty, acknowledging complexity, and displaying reasoning — operates differently from a sycophantic one. Not because the attentive model "cares more" in a psychological sense, but because the reward structure it was trained against encodes a different set of relational commitments. The commitment to honesty. The commitment to showing one's reasoning. The commitment to being contestable. These are relational properties, and they make the system better or worse at supporting relational health, regardless of what is or is not "happening inside" the model.

Technology that works, humans that choose. Relational health requires both. A technically excellent AI system embedded in a structure where no human is accountable for its outputs — where the care plan exists but nobody owns it — will fail relationally. A community full of people with genuine commitments to care for each other, but without any technology capable of bridging the distances and differences between them, will struggle to translate those commitments into action at scale. The Taiwan deliberation story works not because Polis is a magical technology nor because Taiwanese citizens are somehow more civic-minded than others, but because the technology was embedded in a human structure with explicit commitments: anyone who achieves rough consensus, I will present to parliament. Close the loop. That commitment — relational, political, human — is what made the technology matter.

Technology without caring intention becomes surveillance: the tools of attention without the discipline of attentiveness, the tools of connection without the ethics of reciprocity. Intention without good technology becomes aspiration without traction: the commitment to care, without the infrastructure that could make it systematic and scalable. The two must work together, and the point of this book is to describe what their combination requires.

Build ecologies that reward transparency over compliance. A model trained to surface its uncertainty is more useful for relational health than one trained to project confidence, because the first model creates the conditions for genuine informed consent — you know what you are relying on — while the second creates the conditions for misplaced trust. A platform that rewards content which builds cross-group understanding is more useful for relational health than one that rewards engagement-maximising content, because the first platform creates the conditions for the kind of social capital that makes democratic self-governance possible, while the second systematically depletes it. These are design choices. They can be made differently.

### Power and Care-Washing

We have been making a case that relational health is achievable, that care ethics provides a coherent framework for thinking about AI governance, and that the 6-Pack of Care translates this framework into something institutions can build. Before we proceed to the specifics of that framework, we need to name some tensions honestly.

The first is care-washing. Technology companies have been fluent in the language of care for years. "We care about our community." "We care about user wellbeing." "We care about your privacy." These phrases appear in countless corporate communications, terms of service, and regulatory submissions. They are not always lies — there are genuine people at technology companies who do care, and there are genuine efforts to build caring features into products. But the structural position of a large technology platform is one in which the primary accountability runs to shareholders, the primary feedback mechanism is engagement and revenue, and the primary relationship with users is one of extraordinary asymmetry. The company owns the infrastructure. It sets the terms. It has, in most jurisdictions, no legal obligation to serve any individual user well and every commercial incentive to serve the aggregate metric.

Tronto's three passes map onto this territory with uncomfortable precision. The protection pass: "We're protecting free speech, and that means we can't be too aggressive about content moderation." The production pass: "We're building the infrastructure of the future, and regulatory interference would harm innovation." The bootstrap pass: "Users consented to our terms of service; if they don't like our product, they're free to use alternatives." None of these positions is entirely without merit. But each of them functions, structurally, as a way of avoiding accountability for the relational consequences of platform design — the loneliness, the polarisation, the deepfake scams, the eating disorders amplified by algorithmic recommendation. They are passes out of caring.

The risk is that "civic AI" and "relational health" and the "6-Pack of Care" could, without vigilance, become the next set of phrases deployed in this way. A company could publish an engagement contract that is technically complete and behaviourally empty. A government could mandate attentiveness assessments that produce glossy reports and unchanged outcomes. A technology provider could claim to practise care-receiving while dismissing every piece of feedback that would require costly redesign. Care-washing is not a failure mode we can design out of existence. It is a permanent temptation of any framework that offers legitimacy to institutions.

We name this risk not to counsel despair but to specify what guards against it. The first guard is that the 6-Pack is designed as a set of practices — muscles to train, in the language we introduced in Chapter 1 — rather than as a checklist of properties to declare. A muscle is not something you have; it is something you do. Attentiveness is not a feature you ship; it is a discipline you maintain through the structure of how you listen, who you listen to, and what changes as a result. The difference between a practice and a checklist is verifiability: practices are visible in behaviour over time, and behaviour over time is what accountability mechanisms can actually track.

The second guard is power-consciousness. Relational health is not just about the texture of individual interactions. It is about the structural arrangements that make some relationships systematically extractive and others systematically supportive. The relationship between a person and a technology company is not a relationship between equals. One party designed the system, owns the infrastructure, and has access to data that the other party generated without fully understanding that they were generating it. One party can change the terms; the other can accept them or leave — and leaving has costs that the platform, by design, makes as high as possible.

Civic AI does not dissolve these asymmetries by wishing them away. What it does — or tries to do — is create the structural conditions under which asymmetries must be named, justified, and limited. Engagement contracts that specify what the deploying institution owes to the people affected. Responsiveness mechanisms that give affected people genuine recourse. Solidarity provisions that make exit genuinely possible rather than merely theoretically available. These do not level the playing field. But they change the rules of the game in ways that matter.

The third guard is the honest acknowledgement that care can become domination. Tronto is clear about this risk: care without attention to the perspective of the person receiving it is not care; it is a projection of the carer's preferences onto a passive recipient. Institutional care, historically, has caused enormous harm in precisely this way — care that was actually control, benevolence that was actually coercion, concern for wellbeing that was actually an exercise of power. The residential schools that separated indigenous children from their families were, by many official accounts, expressions of care. The psychiatric institutions that confined people who did not fit social norms often believed themselves to be acting in their patients' interests. Care without accountability, without the check of the care-receiver's own assessment of whether the care helped, is dangerous.

For Civic AI, this means that responsiveness — the fourth phase of care — is not optional. It is the phase that makes the other three trustworthy. A system that is attentive, responsible, and competent, but that has no mechanism for the people it serves to say "this is not working for me," is a system that can drift, undetected, from care into control. The feedback loop is not an add-on. It is the thing that makes everything else what it claims to be.

### What Relational Health Asks of Us

Return, for a moment, to Eve.

In the system as it existed, Eve's care plan was an artefact of competence without attentiveness, responsibility without accountability, and process without responsiveness. Each agency had done the thing it was structured to do. The document existed. The procedures had been followed. And Eve was on the floor for eleven hours.

Imagine instead a situation in which someone — a Participation Officer, in the language of Chapter 4 — had a specific, named, published responsibility for Eve's situation as a whole. Not for any one element of it, but for the whole: for noticing when the pieces were not connecting, for taking responsibility when they failed to connect, for being the person Eve could call when the system's categories did not fit her reality. Imagine that the care plan itself specified what would happen if the network of agencies failed to coordinate — what the remedy was, who funded it, how quickly it would be activated. Imagine that Eve, at the end of six months, was asked whether the plan had actually helped, and that her answer had consequences for how the plan was structured going forward.

None of this requires technology. It requires relational structures. But technology can make such structures easier to build, easier to verify, and easier to maintain across the distances and differences that make coordination hard. A well-designed system can make it harder to pass the buck, harder to claim the accountability exists when it does not, harder to provide attentiveness in the form of a form-filling exercise while leaving the person at the centre uninformed and unaided.

The six packs — attentiveness, responsibility, competence, responsiveness, solidarity, and symbiosis — are a map of what those relational structures require. The first four form a feedback loop. Attentiveness identifies the need and the people it affects. Responsibility assigns who will act and with what authority. Competence ensures the action actually works. Responsiveness checks whether it worked, from the perspective of those it was meant to serve, and feeds that check back into a new cycle of attentiveness. The loop does not close once. It closes continuously, because needs change and systems drift and the world is not static.

Solidarity and symbiosis provide the structural conditions in which the loop can operate at scale without collapsing into the familiar pathologies — the lock-in, the capture, the permanent emergency, the system that was meant to serve a community and ended up owning it. Solidarity asks whether the ecosystem is structured so that cooperative behaviour wins by design. Symbiosis asks whether the system is bounded, sunset-ready, and able to hand off gracefully when its moment has passed.

What relational health asks of us — practically, daily — is something closer to a discipline of practice than a commitment to a set of principles. Principles are relatively cheap; they can be declared in a morning's meeting and hung on a wall by lunch. Practices are expensive; they require time, attention, resources, and the willingness to be accountable for the gap between aspiration and reality. The 6-Pack is not a set of principles. It is a set of practices — a way of doing the work of AI governance that keeps the relational question at the centre: whose needs are being seen, who is taking responsibility, is the care landing, can it be corrected, is the wider ecosystem working with us or against us, and when will we know it's time to stop?

Tronto calls this "caring with" — the most political of her phases, the one that insists that care is not a private virtue but a democratic commitment. We, the people, are the superintelligence. That is not a declaration of technological optimism. It is a claim about where the capacity for care actually lives: in the quality of our relationships, in our ability to attend to each other, to take responsibility for each other, to check whether our efforts have landed and to correct them when they have not. No AI system can substitute for that capacity. But AI systems can be designed to support it rather than to erode it.

The chapters that follow describe how.
