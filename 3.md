### The Moment Before

At a crosswalk, drivers slow for a child. No one stops to solve an equation. A need appears; a duty follows. That is attentiveness.

It looks simple from the outside — almost reflexive. But the simplicity is deceptive. The driver who slows does not run a calculation about the pedestrian's probable trajectory or weigh the expected cost of delay against the risk of harm. Something more immediate happens: the child is seen, truly seen, as someone whose life matters. The noticing itself is already a moral act.

Now scale up. An AI system looks at a world full of such crosswalks — workers, rivers, languages, communities recovering from floods, elders who do not use smartphones, night-shift workers whose hours make them invisible to most consultation processes. Within that world, AI can treat each situation as an obstacle, a data point, or a process to be optimised. Or it can treat each as a relationship asking for care. The difference does not begin with the algorithm. It begins with the first look.

Joan Tronto, the political theorist whose work on care ethics underpins this entire book, calls attentiveness "a suspension of one's self-interest, and a capacity genuinely to look from the perspective of the one in need." The opposite is what she names _privileged irresponsibility_: the luxury of not noticing. "One of the great benefits of being in a position of superiority," Tronto writes, "is that one need not exert conscious effort in maintaining that system. Such privileged irresponsibility usually takes the form of complete ignorance of a problem." Attentiveness is the discipline of refusing that luxury — the choice, made again and again, to actually look rather than assume.

This is where the six phases of care begin, and it is in many respects the hardest phase — not because the skill is complex but because the conditions that make attentiveness difficult are structural. The problem is not that AI engineers choose not to notice. The problem is that the systems within which AI is developed reward certain kinds of attention and actively discount others. A model trained on a corpus skewed toward English, toward text, toward the articulate and the connected, will reflect the attentive habits of those who built it. It will notice some crosswalks and miss others.

As Carol Gilligan has observed: "Radical listening holds the potential for transformation because it starts from a place of not knowing and develops the muscle of curiosity." That phrase — the muscle of curiosity — matters enormously for how we think about AI attentiveness. Curiosity is not passive. It is practised. It is built through repeated acts of turning toward the absence rather than assuming the absence means nothing is there.

The design work of this chapter is about building that muscle into systems — creating the conditions in which the discipline of attentiveness can be exercised at scale.

### What Attentiveness Actually Requires

Attentiveness is sometimes confused with data collection — the idea that if a system captures more inputs from more sources, it is therefore more attentive. This confuses breadth of surveillance with quality of listening. A government that records every phone call in its territory is not attentive to its citizens. It is merely watching them. Surveillance is attention without relationship; listening is attention within one.

Four commitments define genuine attentiveness.

The first is to treat relationships as the basic unit of care, not individuals as isolated data points. Some situations make duties visible precisely because of the roles and dependencies that connect people: a city flood-response team is in a relationship with displaced residents, and that relationship generates specific obligations. Genuine listening means providing secure channels for voluntary input — not harvesting data as exhaust, not inferring needs from behavioural signals, but creating conditions in which people can choose to speak and can trust that their speech will matter.

The second is that power must answer questions. Decisions made on the basis of what we think we have heard must be explainable and challengeable. If no one can question the process by which needs were assessed — if the summary is a black box, if the people most affected have no route by which to say "that is not what I meant" — then the attentiveness is incomplete, regardless of how many inputs were collected.

The third concerns precision: we should be precise only when precision helps. We begin with stories and with people. We add numbers when they genuinely clarify — when, for instance, one worker's story can be shown to represent the experience of eight hundred others, and when that scale makes the case for policy action a single voice could not. We update the numbers when reality changes, rather than treating the first measurement as a permanent frame.

The fourth is a rights baseline. This book grounds care ethics within rights-respecting, pluralist democratic institutions. We use the Universal Declaration of Human Rights plus relevant local constitutional rights as the floor for participation — not because abstract rights are prior to relationships, but because rights are the threshold condition for relational standing. Someone whose basic existence is being denied cannot participate as an equal. When claims arrive that try to erase someone's standing — that deny the legitimate presence of a group — those claims are recorded but do not set the agenda. Hospitality has a floor.

These four commitments — relationships first, accountable power, careful precision, and a rights floor — are the grammar of attentiveness: the underlying structure that makes the sentence of care coherent rather than merely well-intentioned.

### What Attentiveness Looks Like in Practice

The most important habit is to look for absences rather than just attending to presences. "We heard nothing from night-shift carers — go find them." Missing voices are not a gap in the data; they are evidence. Their absence tells us something specific: the channels we opened were not the right channels, the timing was wrong, the language was inaccessible. The correct response is to treat the silence as a prompt for investigation.

Hélène Landemore describes this as the test of the "jolly hostess": not merely keeping the door open, but moving through the room to find the person standing alone by the wall and asking the question that only they can answer. The door-open standard is passive. The jolly hostess standard is active: some people will not come in even when the door is open, for reasons that have nothing to do with their desire to participate and everything to do with whether they feel they belong, whether the time works for them, whether past experiences have taught them that their presence will not matter.

This principle can be embedded in system design through jolly-hostess prompt design. When an AI system's governing instructions explicitly require it to act as a bridging host rather than an echo chamber — to represent perspectives of groups whose voices have been quieter, to flag underrepresentation, to surface the uncommon ground — attentiveness becomes structural rather than incidental.

This connects to how AI outputs should work in deliberative contexts. We propose that AI outputs in civic settings be presented as what we call brochures: clean, shareable, editable artefacts that make the AI's reasoning transparent and its sources visible. A brochure is not a private chat message. It is a document a group can look at together, that can be corrected, that links its claims to the inputs it drew on, and that marks where there is disagreement rather than smoothing it into false consensus. When an AI output is a shared artefact, it invites collective deliberation and keeps the relationships between people — rather than the relationship between each person and the system — at the centre.

Two further practices complete the picture. Fair attention means resisting the pull toward the loudest: giving more time to those most affected, and giving explicit weight to smaller, coherent clusters of perspectives. And reading the conflict structure means asking not just what people disagree about but whether disagreements follow a pattern. When the same groups oppose each other on every issue, we are looking at reinforcing cleavages. When people who oppose each other on one question turn out to be allies on another, we have cross-cutting cleavages — and these are the bridges worth finding. The bridging map does not flatten disagreement into a synthetic average; it reveals the structure, and specifically surfaces the people who cross the usual lines.

### One Case: The Flood-Bot

To understand what is at stake when attentiveness is or is not built in, consider the story of a flood response. It is a composite, but every element is drawn from systems we have seen built, or watched fail to be built, in real disaster-response contexts.

A midsize city is hit by a significant flood. Rivers break their banks over a weekend. By Monday morning, thousands are displaced, thousands more are without utilities, and social services are overwhelmed. The city launches a chatbot — the flood-bot — to help people apply for emergency cash assistance.

From the outside, this looks straightforward. The city has data about which areas flooded, a form to fill out, a system for disbursing cash. The chatbot is the interface. Build it quickly, deploy it widely. What could go wrong?

What goes wrong, if the system is not designed with attentiveness, is that the people who most need the assistance are precisely the people the system cannot see.

Elena is a night-shift hospital worker. She sleeps during most hours when public information is disseminated. She does not follow the city's social media accounts — the feeds are in the majority language, not hers. She rents from a private landlord who collects cash and has never provided a formal lease. When the flood came, it took her furniture, her savings (kept in cash because she does not fully trust banks in this country), and the paper documents she had accumulated as proof of where she lives.

Elena has real needs, real harm. And she is, from the perspective of a system designed without attentiveness, almost entirely invisible. She will not find the chatbot through official channels because she does not use those channels. Even if she does find it, the interface is in the majority language, it requires documents she no longer has, and it demands a verified utility bill as proof of residence. It is designed, in effect, for people like the people who designed it.

The flood-bot designed with attentiveness works differently from the beginning.

It begins with listening. People can reach it via voice notes, text messages in any language, and physical kiosks installed in community centres in the most heavily affected neighbourhoods. Kiosks are staffed by volunteers trained to help people without documents. Every contribution stays in its original language, with a human-verified translation beside it, so anyone reviewing the record can check whether the meaning was accurately captured.

Elena finds a kiosk near her neighbourhood. She is tired — just off a night shift — but a volunteer speaks her language and helps her record a voice note explaining her situation. She does not have a lease or utility bills in her name. She has her landlord's contact number and the names of neighbours who can confirm she has lived there for three years. The system is designed to accept this. The attentiveness was built in before Elena arrived.

The mapping step makes Elena's situation visible alongside thousands of others. The team processing inputs sorts needs into categories — housing, wage loss, documentation barriers — and keeps the disagreements visible. Renters and homeowners need fundamentally different kinds of proof. The mapping records this honestly rather than assuming a one-size-fits-all standard.

The receipt mechanism means that when Elena's voice note is processed and summarised, she receives a message through the kiosk system telling her how her situation has been represented and what happens next. There is a button to flag errors. She presses it: the summary describes her as a "renter without documentation" when she actually has documentation of a different kind — neighbours and a landlord who can vouch for her. A human reviewer looks at the correction within four hours and updates the file.

The fair queue gives Elena's application priority review because the system has flagged her as both high-need and low-digital-footprint, which correlates with higher risk of having been underserved by standard channels. The algorithm does not decide her case. It ensures a human reviewer looks at it promptly, rather than processing applications by submission order — which would have systematically disadvantaged people who arrived at the system later.

The public dashboard tracks who has been reached: by neighbourhood, by language, by housing tenure type. On the fourth day it shows that uptake in one postal district is forty per cent of what flood damage estimates would predict, and that the district has a high proportion of residents who speak a minority language not yet represented in the kiosk network. A volunteer team goes out, finds the community, and opens three additional kiosk locations within twenty-four hours.

This is what attentiveness makes possible. Not a perfect system — Elena still faces weeks of difficult bureaucracy, the dashboard still has gaps — but a system structured to notice its own absences and treat those absences as evidence requiring action.

### What Could Go Wrong

Any framework for attentiveness is also a framework for understanding its failure modes. The failures are not random. They follow patterns that recur across contexts.

- **One metric runs the show.** Engagement is up, but trust is down. The most easily measured thing becomes the de facto definition of success, while harder-to-measure things — whether the people most affected felt genuinely heard — go untracked. Fix: Use a small set of balanced measures and rotate them so that no single metric can be gamed without the others detecting the problem.

- **Listening theatre.** The process is beautiful. The reports are glossy. The outcomes are unchanged. Fix: Real decision gates — specific moments at which the output of the listening process is a required input to actual decisions — combined with outside veto power and spot checks by people with no stake in the process appearing to have worked.

- **Loud voices take over.** Well-funded organisations and technically confident actors flood the channel through structural advantage rather than bad faith. Fix: Throttles that limit the volume of input from any single source, fair quotas that ensure all relevant constituencies receive attention, and public dashboards showing whose voices are most and least represented.

- **First movers freeze the frame.** The language established in the first weeks — the categories, the terms, the framing — becomes entrenched, making it harder for perspectives that arrive later to be articulated in their own terms. Fix: Rolling windows that periodically reopen questions the process has tentatively answered, and explicit boosts for late-arriving but important perspectives.

- **False balance.** Treating all views as equally legitimate, including views that deny the basic standing of other participants. Fix: Separate factual disputes from value disagreements; uphold the rights baseline; refuse to extend equivalence to claims designed to erase others' standing rather than contest their position.

- **Fake crowds.** Coordinated, copy-paste comments designed to make a narrow position appear broadly held. Fix: Source checks and rate limits that make it possible to identify coordinated submission patterns, combined with public transparency about the detection methods used.

- **Capture by power.** Oversight mechanisms migrate, over time, toward the actors with the most to lose from genuine scrutiny. Fix: Keep oversight structurally independent, with rulings published, funding transparent, and membership regularly renewed rather than self-perpetuating.

- **Procedural capture.** The format of participation is learned by those with resources to learn it. Fix: Fund participation directly — paid time, childcare, translation, community intermediaries — and track not just who shows up but who is missing.

### How Attentiveness Connects to the Rest

Attentiveness does not operate alone. It is the first phase of a care cycle that depends on all the other phases being functioning and honest, and it is fed by the cycle's later phases when they return information about what was missed or misunderstood.

The relationship with Responsibility (Chapter 4) is the most direct. Attentiveness hands over what it has found — the who, the what, the why, the flags on rights violations, the explicit markers of uncertainty — to the phase that must translate noticing into obligation. Without attentiveness, Responsibility operates in the dark. Without Responsibility, attentiveness is merely observational.

The relationship with Competence (Chapter 5) is about safe learning. Areas where attentiveness has surfaced high uncertainty are precisely the areas where Competence must move carefully, using small safe-to-fail trials rather than confident deployment. Attentiveness flags the caution zones; Competence decides how to move within them.

The relationship with Solidarity (Chapter 7) is about what attentiveness builds over time. When people from different groups observe that the process is genuinely trying to hear all of them — when they see the bridging map rather than a manufactured consensus, when they receive receipts and can correct their representation — they begin to develop the cross-group trust that Solidarity requires.

The relationship with Responsiveness (Chapter 6) is the feedback loop. When care has been given and received, the response — whether the help actually helped — comes back to restart the attentiveness cycle, making it wiser in the next round.

Finally, the relationship with Symbiosis (Chapter 8) operates across the lifecycle of the system itself. When an AI system is retired, it should leave behind the maps, the evaluations, and the receipts it accumulated: a better baseline, a richer picture of who was hard to reach and why, a more honest starting point for the next first look.

### A Closing Image: The Jolly Hostess Who Can Still Say No

Picture a jolly hostess. She is at the door when her guests arrive, welcoming each one by name. She has made space for what they will need — the guest who uses a wheelchair, the guest whose dietary requirements are particular, the guest who arrived from a long journey and needs a moment before they can be sociable.

But she does not stay at the door. She moves through the room. She notices the person standing alone by the wall — the one who has come in but is not yet in the conversation, whose quietness is not contentment but uncertainty about whether they are truly welcome here, whether the things they know and care about are the things this gathering values. She goes to that person. She asks the question — the specific question that only that person can answer, that acknowledges the particular thing they bring. And she listens to the answer.

That is attentiveness. Not passive openness. Active seeking. Not waiting for people to claim their space. Going to the people who have reason to doubt that their space exists.

And because some guests, given the opportunity, will try to erase others — will use their presence to deny others the right to be present, will claim that their standing depends on the delegitimisation of someone else — the hostess also holds a firm rule. Hospitality within a rights-respecting home. The door is open. The welcome is genuine. And some things cannot be done here.

This is the image we return to again and again: not the algorithm that processes the most inputs, not the consultation that attracts the largest turnout, but the host who moves through the room. Teach our systems to be jolly hosts — attentive, not prematurely optimising, actively curious about who is not yet in the conversation — and we will keep more of what is precious, and create more that can be shared.

The need appears. The duty follows. That is where everything begins.
